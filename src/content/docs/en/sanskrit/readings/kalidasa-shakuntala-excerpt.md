## Kālidāsa and Classical Sanskrit Drama

**Kālidāsa** is the most highly esteemed poet and playwright in the history of Classical Sanskrit literature. His dates remain debated, but he is often placed in the Gupta period (roughly the 4th–5th centuries CE). His best-known play, the **Abhijñānaśākuntalam** ("[The Recognition of] Śakuntalā by the Token [of Recognition]," referring to a ring), dramatizes in seven acts the love story of King Duṣyanta and Śakuntalā, the daughter of a hermit, also told within the Mahābhārata. Introduced to European languages in the 19th century — first through William Jones's English translation, then through Goethe's famous praise — it became widely known in the West as the representative masterpiece of classical Indian theater.

Classical Sanskrit drama (*nāṭya*) typically follows the norms laid out in the canonical treatise on dramaturgy, the *Nāṭyaśāstra*: dialogue proceeds mostly in prose, with **verse stanzas such as ślokas** inserted at moments of heightened emotion. The passage below is one such verse, spoken when King Duṣyanta first glimpses Śakuntalā at the hermitage and is captivated by her beauty.

## The Text (Act 1)

```
अनाघ्रातं पुष्पं किसलयमलूनं कररुहैः
अनाविद्धं रत्नं मधु नवमनास्वादितरसम् ।
अखण्डं पुण्यानां फलमिव च तद्रूपमनघं
न जाने भोक्तारं कमिह समुपस्थास्यति विधिः ॥

anāghrātaṃ puṣpaṃ kisalayam alūnaṃ kararuhaiḥ
anāviddhaṃ ratnaṃ madhu navam anāsvāditarasam |
akhaṇḍaṃ puṇyānāṃ phalam iva ca tadrūpam anaghaṃ
na jāne bhoktāraṃ kam iha samupasthāsyati vidhiḥ ||
```

**Translation:** "Like a flower never smelled, a tender sprout never plucked by nails, a jewel never pierced, fresh honey never tasted, an unbroken fruit of accumulated merit — such is her unblemished form. I do not know whom fate will bring here to enjoy it."

## Features of Kāvya Style

This single verse concentrates several techniques typical of Classical Sanskrit poetry (*kāvya*). First, by stringing together five negated adjectives — *anāghrāta* ("unsmelled"), *alūna* ("unplucked"), *anāviddha* ("unpierced"), *anāsvādita-rasa* ("untasted"), *akhaṇḍa* ("unbroken") — the poet uses a technique of **parallel enumeration** to indirectly convey Śakuntalā's untouched purity without ever using a word that simply means "beautiful." The structure stacking five similes (*upamā*) — a flower, a tender sprout, a jewel, honey, and the fruit of merit — exemplifies **alaṃkāra** ("poetic ornamentation"), a central rhetorical technique of kāvya. The whole verse is composed in a long meter called *śārdūlavikrīḍita* (19 syllables per line), whose stately, unhurried rhythm has sometimes been read as itself enacting the slow passage of time as the king stands transfixed.

## The Significance of Śakuntalā as Drama

Śakuntalā has been praised for far more than being a simple romance: it engages with memory and forgetting, the contrast between the natural world (the forest hermitage) and the royal court, and questions of fate — this verse's word *vidhi* ("fate/providence") being one small trace of that theme. Later in the play, Duṣyanta loses all memory of his marriage to Śakuntalā because of a curse, and only regains it through a ring — the "token of recognition" (*abhijñāna*) — that gives the play its title.
