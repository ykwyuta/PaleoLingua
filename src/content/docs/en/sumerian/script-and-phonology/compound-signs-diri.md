## When signs "merge"

Most of the signs covered so far have been single, standalone symbols. But cuneiform also has cases where two or more basic signs combine into a single compound symbol, and that combination as a whole takes on a unique reading distinct from either component sign. Such compounds are called "diri ligatures" (from the Sumerian word `diri`, "to exceed, be extra" — a term borrowed from the sign whose own compound writing gave the category its name). In transliteration, the component signs are conventionally shown joined by `×` (superimposed) or `.` (juxtaposed).

## The classic example: KA×GAR = "to eat"

The most famous diri compound overlays the sign for `gar` ("food," "bread") inside the sign for `ka` ("mouth"), producing the verb `gu7`, "to eat."

```
𒅗          𒃻            𒅥
ka          gar           gu7
カ          ガル           グ
mouth        food          (compound) to eat
```

The compound sign is read neither as `ka` ("mouth") nor as `gar` ("food") — the merged symbol as a whole carries an entirely new reading, `gu7` ("to eat"). The likely inspiration behind the shape is a simple pictorial idea: food inside a mouth.

## Why compounds like this arose

In the earliest pictographic stage of the script, scribes combined existing signs to convey new concepts visually. Overlaying "mouth" and "food" to depict "eating" is a classic example of this strategy — actions and abstract concepts that a single sign couldn't fully capture were expressed instead by combining multiple signs.

## Spotting them in transliteration

Diri compounds are usually marked in transliterated text through capitalization and the `×`/`.` notation showing which signs combine. But when read aloud, only the single reading assigned to the whole compound (`gu7` in the example above) is pronounced — the individual components (`ka`, `gar`) are not read out separately.
