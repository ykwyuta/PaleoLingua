## A simple syllable structure: avoiding consonant clusters

[Cuneiform as a Syllabary](/en/sumerian/script-and-phonology/cuneiform-syllabary/) showed that cuneiform syllabograms fall into four types: V, CV, VC, and CVC. That typology also reflects the syllable structure of the Sumerian language itself: a Sumerian syllable almost never allows a cluster of consonants within it (unlike, say, English "str-"), and instead follows a relatively simple pattern of consonants separated by vowels.

## Word-final consonant weakening

One of the best-known phonotactic tendencies in Sumerian is that a consonant at the end of a word (or phrase) tends not to be clearly pronounced — or drops altogether — unless immediately followed by a vowel. This isn't limited to one particular suffix; it's considered a general tendency across the language's phonological system.

The alternation between `-ak` and `-a` already seen in [The Genitive -ak](/en/sumerian/grammar/genitive-case-ak/) is a specific instance of this broader tendency, showing up in a single grammatical element (the genitive suffix).

```
𒂍 𒈗𒅗
e2  lugal-ak
e2  lugal-ak
house  king-GEN (followed by a vowel)
"the house of the king"
```

```
𒂍 𒈗𒆷
e2  lugal-la
e2  lugal-la
house  king-GEN (phrase-final; the *k* drops and the preceding consonant lengthens)
"the house of the king"
```

## Why this matters: don't be fooled by spelling differences

Knowing about this word-final weakening lets you recognize that the same grammatical element (the same case, the same root) can surface with a different spelling depending on context — a mere pronunciation variant, not a different grammatical element. Conversely, when reading a Sumerian text, be careful not to jump to the conclusion that a spelling difference at a word's end always signals a different grammatical element.

## Summary of the syllable structure

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Consonant clusters | Not generally permitted within a syllable (syllables combine as V, CV, VC, or CVC) |
| Word-final consonants | Tend to weaken or drop unless followed by a vowel-initial element |
| Effect on spelling | The same morpheme can appear in different transliterated spellings (e.g., -ak / -la) depending on what follows |
