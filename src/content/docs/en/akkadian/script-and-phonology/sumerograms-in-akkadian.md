## An Akkadian sentence spelled in Sumerian?

Open an Akkadian tablet and you will frequently find a word, in an unmistakably Akkadian sentence, spelled with a Sumerian-derived logogram (a "Sumerogram") instead of Akkadian syllables. The word for "king," for instance, is pronounced `šarrum` in Akkadian, but tablets very often simply write the Sumerian logogram for "king":

<p class="cuneiform">𒈗</p>

```
LUGAL
šarrum "king" — the Sumerian logogram used to spell an Akkadian word
```

A reader encountering `LUGAL` does not pronounce it as Sumerian "lugal" — they read it aloud as the Akkadian word `šarrum`. In other words, `LUGAL` encodes only the *meaning* "king"; the reader has to supply the actual Akkadian pronunciation from grammatical knowledge.

## Why did scribes write this way?

Two main reasons are usually given.

1. **Economy.** Spelling `šarrum` syllable-by-syllable takes four signs (`ša-ar-ru-um`), whereas the logogram `LUGAL` takes just one. Given the labor of impressing signs into wet clay, this was a real saving of effort.
2. **Scribal tradition.** Cuneiform was originally developed to write Sumerian, and Sumerian remained the prestige language of scholarship, religion, and administration in Mesopotamia long after it ceased to be spoken. Akkadian scribes were trained systematically in Sumerian logograms at scribal school (the *edubba*), and reusing that inventory when writing Akkadian was a natural extension of their training.

## How does a reader recover the Akkadian word form?

Because a Sumerogram by itself indicates only meaning, scribes needed a way to convey grammatical information — case, number, person — to the reader. They did this by attaching a "phonetic complement": one or more syllabic signs, usually right after the logogram, spelling out the ending of the intended Akkadian word.

Adding the nominative ending `-um` as a phonetic complement after `LUGAL` gives:

<p class="cuneiform">𒈗𒌝</p>

```
LUGAL-um
šarrum "king" (masc. nom. sg.) — -um is a phonetic complement spelling out the nominative ending
```

The `-um` tells the reader unambiguously that `LUGAL` here is meant as the nominative form `šarrum`. Swapping the phonetic complement changed the case: `LUGAL-im` (→ `šarrim`) for the genitive, `LUGAL-am` (→ `šarram`) for the accusative.

## Some common Sumerograms

Here are three logograms in everyday use in Akkadian texts.

| Sumerogram | Cuneiform | Akkadian equivalent | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| LUGAL | 𒈗 | šarrum | king |
| E₂ | 𒂍 | bītum | house |
| DINGIR | 𒀭 | ilum | god |

Take `E2` "house":

<p class="cuneiform">𒂍</p>

```
E2
bītum "house" — a Sumerian logogram used to spell an Akkadian word
```

The same word spelled syllabically would be `bi-tum` — an example of the "broken" (syllabic) spelling covered in [Transliteration Conventions](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/transliteration-conventions/).

<p class="cuneiform">𒁉𒌈</p>

```
bi-tum
bītum "house" (masc., nom. sg.)
```

And `DINGIR` "god":

<p class="cuneiform">𒀭</p>

```
DINGIR
ilum "god" — a Sumerian logogram used to spell an Akkadian word
```

## Choosing between logograms and syllabic spelling

On real tablets, the same word may be spelled either way — with a Sumerogram or with Akkadian syllables — and the choice depended on genre, period, and individual scribal habit. Administrative documents and royal inscriptions tend to favor logograms for their economy, while contexts where precise grammatical detail mattered (poetic meter, or passages meant to display case endings clearly) more often use syllabic spelling.
