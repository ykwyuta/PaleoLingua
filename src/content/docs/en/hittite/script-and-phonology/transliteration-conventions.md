## Why Transliteration Conventions Matter

Since cuneiform itself is essentially unreadable to non-specialists, Hittitology relies on **transliteration** — rendering cuneiform in the Latin alphabet — as the standard tool for research and teaching. Much of this notation is shared with Assyriology, and it is designed so that a single glance at a transliterated string tells the reader whether a given element is a syllabic sign, a Sumerogram, or a determinative.

## Basic Conventions at a Glance

| Notation | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Lowercase + hyphen | Boundary between syllabic signs | e-eš-zi ("he/she is") |
| Roman capitals | Sumerogram | LUGAL "king" |
| Capitals joined by periods | A compound Sumerogram made of several signs | LUGAL.GAL "great king" |
| Italic capitals | Akkadogram | *ANA* "to, for" |
| Superscript | Determinative | ᴰIŠTAR (the goddess Ishtar) |
| Subscript index number | Distinguishes signs that share a reading | da, dá (=da₂), dà (=da₃) |

## Sign Boundaries and the Hyphen

When several syllabic signs make up a single word, a **hyphen** marks the boundary between them. The word for "he/she is, exists," for example, is transliterated e-eš-zi, immediately showing that it is composed of the three syllabic signs e, eš, and zi.

## Determinatives (Superscripts)

Personal names, divine names, place names, and occupational titles are often preceded (or followed) by a **determinative** — a sign indicating the semantic category of the word. In transliteration this is shown as a superscript. The determinative D (from Sumerian DINGIR "god"), for instance, appears as a superscript before a divine name: ᴰIŠTAR (the goddess Ishtar). Determinatives themselves are thought not to have been pronounced. See [Determinatives in Hittite](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/determinatives-in-hittite/) for more.

## Distinguishing Homophonous Signs: Index Numbers

Cuneiform often has multiple signs that share the same phonetic value but differ in shape. To distinguish these in transliteration, scholars conventionally use accent marks or subscript index numbers, ordered by relative frequency.

```
da   (no index — the most common sign with this value)
dá   (= da₂, the second most common sign with the same value)
dà   (= da₃, a third distinct sign shape)
```

These index numbers do not indicate differences in pronunciation; they are purely a philological device for recording which specific sign shape appears on the original tablet.

## Marking Damage, Restoration, and Scribal Error

Because tablets are frequently damaged, transliteration also uses symbols to indicate the physical condition of the text.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `[ ]` (square brackets) | Text lost to damage, restored based on context |
| `⸢ ⸣` (half brackets) | Text that is partially damaged but still legible |
| `< >` (angle brackets) | A sign the scribe is thought to have accidentally omitted, supplied by the editor |
| `<< >>` (double angle brackets) | A sign the scribe is thought to have mistakenly written in excess |

These conventions let readers clearly distinguish what is actually preserved on the tablet from what a modern scholar has reconstructed or supplied.
