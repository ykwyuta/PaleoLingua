## What Is a Saṃyuktākṣara?

Sanskrit is extremely rich in **consonant clusters** — sequences of two or more consonants with no vowel between them. Rather than simply writing the consonant letters side by side, Devanāgarī orthography, as a rule, merges multiple consonant letters into a single visual ligature called a **saṃyuktākṣara** (संयुक्ताक्षर, "joined syllable-sign"), or **conjunct consonant**. This mechanism is simply an application of the **virama** (्) introduced in [An Overview of the Devanāgarī Script](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/devanagari-script-overview/).

## How Conjuncts Are Formed

The principle is simple: consonant letter A is stripped of its vowel (via the virama) and joined directly to the following consonant letter B. In practice, the virama symbol itself usually does not appear visibly; instead, the shapes of the two consonant letters visually merge and simplify.

| Stage | Form | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Two independent consonants (with vowels) | क + त | ka, ta (two separate syllables) |
| 2. Virama suppresses the vowel | क् + त | k (no vowel) + ta |
| 3. Merged into a conjunct | क्त | kta (a single ligature glyph) |

In क्त (kta), as in many conjuncts, the vertical stroke of the first consonant letter is dropped so that it connects directly into the following letter. Conjunct shapes follow certain recurring patterns, but the degree of simplification varies considerably depending on which consonants combine, so beginners generally need to learn many shapes individually.

## Common Conjunct Examples

| Conjunct | Components | IAST | Example word |
|---|---|---|---|
| क्ष | क् + ष | kṣa | क्षत्रिय kṣatriya "the warrior class" |
| ज्ञ | ज् + ञ | jña | ज्ञान jñāna "knowledge" |
| त्र | त् + र | tra | पुत्र putra "son" |
| द्व | द् + व | dva | द्वार dvāra "door" |
| श्व | श् + व | śva | अश्व aśva "horse" |
| स्त | स् + त | sta | स्तुति stuti "praise, hymn" |
| ह्म | ह् + म | hma | ब्रह्मन् brahman (the same element appears in ब्राह्मण brāhmaṇa) |

Among these, क्ष (kṣa) and ज्ञ (jña) are especially well known as **irregular conjuncts**: their shapes have simplified into forms that bear little obvious resemblance to the original two letters, so beginners essentially have to memorize them as unique symbols. By contrast, conjuncts such as त्र (tra) and द्व (dva) retain more of their component shapes and are easier to parse.

## Clusters of Three or More Consonants

Sanskrit is not unusual in stringing together three or more consonants. For example, in a word like **कार्त्स्न्य** *kārtsnya* "totality," a sequence like *rtsny* piles up multiple consonants. In such cases, the conjunct can stack into an even more elaborate shape (and in practice, manuscript and print conventions sometimes vary on how much to merge versus separate). Conjunct complexity also varies between manuscripts and printed editions, and the elaborate rules of [sandhi](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/sandhi-external-internal/) can create fresh consonant clusters at word boundaries, adding further to the variety of conjuncts one encounters.

## Transliterating Conjuncts

In Latin-script transliteration schemes such as IAST, there is no need to merge a conjunct into a single symbol — the component consonant letters are simply written in sequence.

| Devanāgarī conjunct | IAST transliteration |
|---|---|
| क्ष | kṣ (with a vowel: kṣa, etc.) |
| ज्ञ | jñ (with a vowel: jña, etc.) |
| त्र | tr (with a vowel: tra, etc.) |

In other words, information that Devanāgarī expresses through visual merging (ligature formation) is expressed in IAST transliteration as a plain sequence of letters. Understanding this correspondence takes the intimidation out of conjuncts, letting you quickly decompose them into their component consonants via the transliteration.
