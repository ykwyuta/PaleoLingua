## What Is Plene Writing?

Hittite cuneiform texts frequently spell the same vowel sign twice in a row. This is called **plene writing** (from Latin *plene*, "fully"), and most scholars take it to be one of the main ways scribes indicated a long vowel.

```
pár-ku-i-e-eš
"pure" (adjective) - NOM.PL
"the pure (ones)"
```

In the example above, the doubled -e- (in -e-eš) is a case of plene writing, suggesting that the vowel in this position was probably a long ē.

## An Inconsistent Convention

A key feature of plene writing is that it is **not applied consistently**. The very same word or stem may be spelled plene in one manuscript and with a single vowel sign in another — or even in a different passage of the same manuscript. This means the presence or absence of plene writing alone cannot be used as a mechanical test for vowel length; scholars must compare multiple manuscripts and multiple attestations of a word.

## Environments Where Plene Writing Is More Common

Accumulated research has identified some tendencies in where plene writing tends to appear:

- It appears relatively more often in accented (stressed) syllables.
- Certain morphemes (some case endings or verbal endings) show a conventionally fixed pattern of plene writing.
- Some scholars have noted that plene writing seems to become more frequent in New Hittite manuscripts, though this varies by scribe and text type and should not be over-generalized.

## Vowel Length and Grammatical Distinctions

Vowel length is not merely a phonetic detail — in some cases it appears to be bound up with grammatical distinctions. For certain nouns and verbs, a short-vowel form versus a long-vowel (plene-written) form has been proposed as a clue distinguishing case, person, or number. Because of the inconsistency described above, however, how confidently vowel length can be established in any individual case is a point on which Hittitologists sometimes disagree.
