## The Standard Babylonian Version and Sîn-lēqi-unninni

The Epic of Gilgamesh survives in several versions, but the best known today is the twelve-tablet **Standard Babylonian version**. Tradition attributes its compilation and editing to the scholar-priest **Sîn-lēqi-unninni**, who is said to have drawn together older Old Babylonian material and the independent Sumerian Gilgamesh poems (see [Introducing the Sumerian Gilgamesh Poems](/en/sumerian/readings/gilgamesh-intro/)) into a single unified narrative, probably during the Middle Babylonian period (c. 1300–1000 BCE).

Most surviving copies of the Standard Babylonian version come from the royal library that the Neo-Assyrian king **Ashurbanipal** (7th century BCE) assembled at Nineveh. This library was uncovered in excavations by Austen Henry Layard and Hormuzd Rassam in the 1850s, and vast quantities of its tablets were shipped to the British Museum. In 1872, the British Museum researcher George Smith famously recognized that one of these tablets — the flood narrative of Tablet XI — closely paralleled the flood story of the Hebrew Bible, a discovery that caused a sensation.

## The Opening Line: "He Who Saw the Deep"

Tablet I of the Standard Babylonian version opens with the following line. Ancient scribes frequently catalogued a literary work by its opening words, so this line effectively served as the epic's ancient title.

<p class="cuneiform">𒊭 𒈾𒀝𒁀 𒄿𒈬𒊒</p>

```
ša           na-aq-ba      i-mu-ru
[relative]   deep-ACC      saw(3ms)-SBJV
"He who saw the Deep"
```

## Word-by-Word Analysis

| Transliteration | Cuneiform | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ša | 𒊭 | Relative particle (invariable for gender/number/case) | who, that, the one who |
| na-aq-ba | 𒈾𒀝𒁀 | naqb- (noun stem "the deep, the totality of things") + -a (accusative ending) | the deep (acc.) |
| i-mu-ru | 𒄿𒈬𒊒 | ī- (G-stem preterite of amāru "to see") + -mur (root √'MR) + -u (subordinate-clause ending) | (he) saw |

## Grammatical Notes

- **The relative particle `ša`**: An invariable relative particle showing no agreement for gender, number, or case, meaning "who/that/the one who." It can form a headless relative clause — a nominal phrase with no separate antecedent — and that is exactly what it does here: the whole line functions as a subject phrase, "the one who saw the deep." See [Relative Clauses with ša](/en/akkadian/grammar/relative-clauses-sa/).
- **The subordinate-clause ending `-u`**: Unlike a verb in an ordinary main clause, a verb inside a relative clause introduced by `ša` takes a special ending in `-u` — the so-called subjunctive form. The final `-u` on `īmuru` is this ending.
- **The long vowel of `īmuru`**: The verb `amāru`, "to see," has a glottal stop (aleph) as its first root consonant. In the G-stem preterite, the loss of this consonant produces compensatory lengthening, yielding `īmur`, "he saw." Here the subordinate-clause ending `-u` is added, giving `īmuru`.
- **The accusative `naqba`**: The accusative singular of `naqbu`, "the deep, the totality of subterranean waters, hence 'everything.'" Standard Babylonian has lost mimation on the case endings, so the form is `naqba` rather than the Old Babylonian `naqbam`.
