## Background: Mesopotamian Divination Literature

Ancient Mesopotamians believed that the gods revealed signs about the future through every kind of event in the world — the behavior of animals, the movements of celestial bodies, physical abnormalities, and the content of dreams. The literature that recorded and systematized these signs is known as **omen literature**. Divination was no marginal superstition; it was a serious intellectual practice pursued at every level of society, from the king's policy decisions down to the concerns of ordinary daily life. Professional diviners (the **bārû**) often held important positions as royal advisors.

Among the many divinatory techniques, one of the most important was **extispicy** — the inspection of the liver and other internal organs of a sacrificed sheep, whose features were read for good or ill omens. Specially made clay models of livers, apparently used as teaching aids, have even been discovered by archaeologists. Another major branch was **celestial divination**, based on observations of the moon, sun, planets, and weather phenomena. The largest compilation of celestial omen lore is the series known as **Enūma Anu Enlil** ("When the gods Anu and Enlil...," a title drawn from its opening line), a vast compendium surviving on dozens of cuneiform tablets.

## The "If X, Then Y" Structure

Omen texts share a common grammatical structure consisting of a **protasis** and an **apodosis**. The protasis begins with the conjunction *šumma* ("if") and states the observed sign; the apodosis follows and states the fate that sign portends. This "if X, then Y" pattern is the same basic conditional structure found throughout the [Code of Hammurabi](/en/akkadian/readings/hammurabi-code-prologue/)'s individual laws.

One well-attested category within the genre concerns the behavior of animals in or around a house or city — for instance, a dog entering a private home. Below is an example sentence illustrating the typical grammar and vocabulary of this type of omen (composed to show the standard form of such omens, rather than a verbatim quotation of one specific numbered tablet).

<p class="cuneiform">𒋳𒈠 𒆗𒁍𒌝 𒀀𒈾 𒁉𒀉 𒀀𒉿𒅆 𒄿𒊒𒌒</p>

```
šum-ma      kal-bu-um    a-na    bi-it            a-wi-lim       i-ru-ub
if          dog          to      house-(constr.)   man-GEN        enter-PRET.3MS
```

"If a dog enters a man's house" — *šumma* 𒋳𒈠 ("if") introduces the entire protasis; *kalbum* 𒆗𒁍𒌝 ("dog") is the subject; *bīt awīlim* 𒁉𒀉 𒀀𒉿𒅆 ("a man's house," construct-state noun + genitive) names the destination of the verb *īrub* 𒄿𒊒𒌒 ("he entered," preterite of *erēbum* "to enter"). An apodosis stating what the dog's visit portends would normally follow.

## Vocabulary Breakdown

| Transliteration | Cuneiform | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| šum-ma | 𒋳𒈠 | šumma (conditional conjunction) | if |
| kal-bu-um | 𒆗𒁍𒌝 | kalbum (stem kalb- + nominative -um) | dog |
| i-ru-ub | 𒄿𒊒𒌒 | 3ms preterite (iprus-type) of erēbum ("to enter") | he entered |

## The Role of Divination in Mesopotamian Intellectual and Religious Life

Omen texts were not merely a record of superstition but the product of a systematic intellectual effort to observe the world and detect patterns within it. Accumulating and compiling thousands of sign-and-outcome pairs is not "science" in the modern sense, but it shares with astronomy and medical observation a commitment to **systematic, observation-based recording**. In particular, the celestial data compiled in Enūma Anu Enlil is recognized as one of the foundations on which the later, more precise Babylonian astronomy was built.
