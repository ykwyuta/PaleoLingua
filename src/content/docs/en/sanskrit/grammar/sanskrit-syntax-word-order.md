As earlier articles have shown, Sanskrit case marking is extraordinarily well developed (see [The Functions of the Case System](/en/sanskrit/grammar/case-system-functions/)). This article looks at how that rich case marking shapes syntax, especially word order and clause combination.

## Basic Word Order: SOV

Sanskrit's unmarked (neutral, unemphasized) word order is generally described as **SOV** — subject, object, verb. This order is thought to be inherited from Proto-Indo-European and shows up in later languages such as Latin and parts of the Germanic branch as well.

```
रामः फलं खादति।
rāmaḥ phalaṃ khādati.
Rāma-NOM fruit-ACC eat-3SG.PRES
"Rāma eats the fruit." (the standard S-O-V order)
```

## Free Word Order Enabled by Case Marking

Because case endings uniquely determine each noun's or pronoun's role in the sentence — subject or object, instrument or beneficiary, and so on — actual Sanskrit word order is remarkably free. The sentence above retains the same grammatical roles and basic meaning no matter how the words are reordered.

```
फलं रामः खादति।    (O-S-V)
फलं खादति रामः।    (O-V-S)
खादति रामः फलं।    (V-S-O)
```

All of these are grammatically well formed and convey the same proposition, "Rāma eats the fruit." Differences in word order, however, often reflect differences in **information structure** — what counts as the topic versus new information, and what is being emphasized. As a general tendency, an element placed at the beginning of the sentence tends to be highlighted as the topic or a point of contrast, while an element placed close to the verb tends to be emphasized as new information.

This free word order is a direct consequence of the rich case marking discussed in [An Overview of Sanskrit Noun Declension](/en/sanskrit/grammar/noun-declension-overview/), and it becomes especially important in verse, where word order must also accommodate poetic meter (chandas).

## The Position of Adjectives and Modifiers

Adjectives tend to precede the noun they modify, but this too is fairly flexible, and in verse an adjective following its noun is not unusual. Genitive possessive expressions likewise typically precede the noun they modify.

```
सुन्दरः पुष्पः / पुष्पः सुन्दरः
sundaraḥ puṣpaḥ / puṣpaḥ sundaraḥ
"a beautiful flower" (grammatical whether the adjective comes before or after)
```

## Postpositional Elements

Sanskrit has almost no true prepositions of the kind typical of many Indo-European languages; instead, the case endings themselves carry meanings a preposition would otherwise supply ("in," "from," and so on). A handful of adverbial particles (such as saha "with") behave as postpositions, following the noun they govern rather than preceding it (since the case itself already carries the meaning, these function less as true prepositions than as adverbial particles reinforcing the case's meaning).

```
रामेण सह गच्छामि।
rāmeṇa saha gacchāmi.
Rāma-INS with go-1SG.PRES
"I go with Rāma." (saha follows rāmeṇa)
```

## Clause Combination: Participles and the Locative Absolute

As seen in [Participles in Sanskrit](/en/sanskrit/grammar/participles-in-sanskrit/), Sanskrit favors combining clauses through participles, the absolutive (-tvā), and the locative absolute construction, rather than through finite subordinate clauses of the kind English builds with "when" or "after."

```
सूर्ये उदिते सः गच्छति।
sūrye udite saḥ gacchati.
sun-LOC risen-LOC he-NOM go-3SG.PRES
"When the sun has risen, he goes." (an adverbial clause via the locative absolute)
```

In this way, a subordinate circumstance with a subject different from the main clause is typically expressed via the locative absolute, while a sequence of actions sharing the main clause's subject is expressed via the absolutive (-tvā) — both more characteristically Sanskrit than reaching for a finite subordinate clause.

## The Correlative Construction: yad~tad

When a finite subordinate clause is needed — especially a relative clause — Sanskrit uses a **correlative construction** pairing the relative pronoun **यद् yad** ("which/who") with the demonstrative pronoun **तद् tad** ("that"). Unlike the English relative pronoun, this construction places a pronoun in both the subordinate and the main clause.

```
यः नरः वदति सः मम मित्रम्।
yaḥ naraḥ vadati saḥ mama mitram.
who-NOM man-NOM speak-3SG.PRES that-NOM my friend
"The man who is speaking is my friend."
```

The typical pattern for a Sanskrit relative clause is for the relative clause (yaḥ naraḥ vadati "the man who is speaking") to come first, followed by the main clause containing the correlative demonstrative (saḥ "that one") that picks it back up.
