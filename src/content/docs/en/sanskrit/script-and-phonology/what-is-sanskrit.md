## Sanskrit at a Glance

Sanskrit (संस्कृत *saṃskṛta*, "refined, perfected [speech]") is a classical language of South Asia with over three thousand years of continuous literary use, and it preserves **one of the oldest well-documented stages of the Indo-European language family**. Nearly the entire traditional corpus of South Asian ritual, philosophical, literary, and scientific knowledge (including grammar, astronomy, and medicine) was recorded in Sanskrit, making it indispensable for understanding classical South Asian civilization.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Region used | South Asia (chiefly the modern India, Pakistan, and Nepal region), later spreading to Southeast Asia along with Buddhism and Hinduism |
| Attested period | c. 1500 BCE (the oldest layers of the *Ṛgveda*) to the present (still used as a scholarly and liturgical language) |
| Family | Indo-Aryan branch (Old Indo-Aryan) of the Indo-Iranian branch of Indo-European |
| Script | Historically written in many scripts of the Brāhmī family; the modern standard is Devanāgarī |
| Basic word order | Basically SOV (subject–object–verb), though word order is relatively free since case marking carries grammatical relations |
| Typology | A highly inflected language with three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), three numbers (singular, dual, plural), and an eight-case noun system, alongside an elaborate verbal conjugation system |
| Codifying grammar | Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī* (c. 4th century BCE) fixed the norms of Classical Sanskrit |

## The Indo-Iranian Branch and Indo-European

Sanskrit forms the Indo-Aryan branch of Indo-Iranian together with Avestan (the liturgical language of ancient Iran), and Indo-Iranian in turn is one branch of the larger Indo-European family. Sanskrit is thought to descend from the same proto-language (Proto-Indo-European) as Latin, Greek, the Germanic languages, the Slavic languages, and many others, and this kinship is clearly visible in its case system, its verbal morphology, and core vocabulary — compare Sanskrit *pitṛ* "father" with Latin *pater* and English *father*. Sanskrit's importance to comparative linguistics as a discipline is historic: Sir William Jones's 1786 observation of striking similarities between Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin is often credited as the founding moment of comparative philology. Because Sanskrit preserves many archaic phonological and morphological features, it remains one of the most important languages for reconstructing Proto-Indo-European.

## Vedic and Classical Sanskrit

The history of Sanskrit is usually divided into two broad stages.

| Stage | Approximate period | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Vedic Sanskrit | c. 1500–500 BCE | The language of the four Vedas, beginning with the *Ṛgveda*. Retains a [pitch accent system (svara)](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/vedic-accent-svara/) and shows more archaic phonology and morphology than the Classical stage |
| Classical Sanskrit | c. 4th century BCE onward | The language codified by Pāṇini's grammar. The language of the great epics (*Mahābhārata*, *Rāmāyaṇa*), of drama and poetry (e.g. Kālidāsa), and of philosophical and scientific literature, still in scholarly and liturgical use today |

Vedic and Classical Sanskrit are best understood as two ends of a continuum rather than separate languages: Classical Sanskrit is essentially the earlier, more variable language "frozen" into a norm by codifying grammar.

## Script — Devanāgarī and What Came Before It

The script most commonly used to write Sanskrit today is [Devanāgarī](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/devanagari-script-overview/), but this script was never exclusive to Sanskrit. Historically, Sanskrit was recorded in numerous regional scripts descended from Brāhmī — including Grantha, Śāradā, Bengali, and the Grantha-family scripts of Tamil-speaking regions. Devanāgarī only became the de facto standard in the early modern period. Given the immense importance of oral transmission (especially the recitation tradition for Vedic texts), Sanskrit is in many ways a language for which precise oral transmission long mattered more than any particular script.

## Why Sanskrit Matters

Sanskrit's importance rests on several pillars. First, the majority of the ritual and philosophical literature of Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism was composed in Sanskrit, making it a key to South Asian intellectual history. Second, Pāṇini's grammar is a landmark in the history of linguistics, often praised for a descriptive precision that anticipated 20th-century structural linguistics by more than two thousand years. Third, from a comparative-linguistic standpoint, Sanskrit — and Vedic Sanskrit in particular — is one of the most important sources for reconstructing Proto-Indo-European phonology and morphology; phenomena such as [vowel gradation (guṇa and vṛddhi)](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/vowel-gradation-guna-vrddhi/) have profoundly shaped the study of ablaut across the Indo-European languages.

## Surviving Literature

The surviving body of Sanskrit literature is vast. It includes the Vedic corpus (hymns, ritual manuals, forest treatises, and philosophical Upaniṣads), the two great epics (*Mahābhārata* and *Rāmāyaṇa*), the Purāṇas, classical drama and poetry represented above all by Kālidāsa, narrative literature such as the *Pañcatantra*, the grammatical tradition beginning with Pāṇini, and the enormous Buddhist and Jain textual traditions.
