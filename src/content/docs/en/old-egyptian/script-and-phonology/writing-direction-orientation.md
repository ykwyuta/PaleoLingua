## Hieroglyphs have no fixed writing direction

Unlike an alphabetic system that always runs in one fixed direction, hieroglyphic writing could be laid out **right-to-left, left-to-right, or top-to-bottom in vertical columns**. Scribes chose freely among these depending on the layout of an inscription or aesthetic balance. This means a learner's first encounter with a real inscription — on a temple wall or stela — can be genuinely disorienting: where do you even start reading?

## The rule for figuring out direction: "read toward the faces"

Fortunately, there's a simple rule. Signs depicting people or animals (a seated figure, a bird, a snake) are always drawn **facing one direction or the other**. The rule is: **you read toward the direction those signs are facing** — that is, you start reading from the side the signs' gaze points away from, and move toward where their faces point.

For example, if all the signs face left, the text reads right-to-left (the reader starts on the right and moves left, following the direction the signs face). If the signs face right, the text reads left-to-right. This is one of the very first principles taught to students of Egyptology.

## The same rule applies to vertical columns

The same orientation rule applies to text arranged in vertical columns (read top to bottom within each column). The columns themselves can be arranged right-to-left or left-to-right, and which column to read first is again determined by which way the human/animal signs within it are facing.

## Honorific transposition

Hieroglyphic writing has a distinctive convention where **reverence outranks grammatical word order**: the name of a god or a royal title is frequently written at the **very beginning** of a word or phrase, regardless of where it would actually fall grammatically. This practice is called **honorific transposition**.

A classic example is the royal title **nsw** ("King of Upper Egypt," part of the full royal titulary). Although the word is analyzed/pronounced in an order along the lines of s-w-n (or a related root), scribes conventionally placed **the sign symbolizing royal authority — the sedge plant, Gardiner M23, not included in this article's verified glyph set and so not shown here — at the very front**, followed by the remaining phonetic elements. Here it is shown using only uniliteral signs to spell the sound:

<p class="hieroglyph">𓈖𓋴𓅱</p>

```
nsw
king (specifically "King of Upper Egypt"; traditionally written with the royal element placed first, e.g. sw-t-n order, as an example of "honorific transposition")
```

The order signs actually appear in an inscription, then, doesn't always match the grammatical or phonological structure of the word — an important thing to keep in mind when reading hieroglyphic texts. Honorific transposition reflects scribes expressing reverence for kings and gods through the very physical arrangement of the signs.
