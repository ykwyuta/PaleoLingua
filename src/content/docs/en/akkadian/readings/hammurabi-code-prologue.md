## Hammurabi and the Stele of the Code

**Hammurabi** (Ḫammu-rapi) was the sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon, reigning roughly from 1792 to 1750 BCE (Middle Chronology). The law collection that bears his name, known as the Code of Hammurabi, consists of 282 provisions inscribed in cuneiform on a black diorite stele approximately 2.25 meters tall.

The stele was likely originally erected in a public space or temple precinct in Babylon, but around the 12th century BCE it was carried off as war booty to Susa (in modern Khuzestan, Iran) by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte I following a campaign against Babylon. French archaeologists (the expedition led by Jacques de Morgan) rediscovered the stele at Susa in 1901–1902, and it is now held at the Louvre Museum in Paris. The relief carved at the top of the stele depicts Hammurabi receiving the rod and ring, symbols of royal authority, from the sun-god and god of justice **Šamaš** — the same deity whose name gives PaleoLingua's own mascot his.

Before the 282 individual provisions of the code proper comes a lengthy **prologue**, and after them an **epilogue**; both are composed in an elevated literary style quite unlike the terse legal formulas of the provisions themselves, and both enumerate Hammurabi's pious works and the cities he benefited. The prologue opens with an account of how the gods charged Hammurabi with the mission of establishing justice — told as an extraordinarily long single sentence built from a chain of subordinate clauses spanning many lines.

## The Opening of the Prologue

The prologue's opening words invoke Anu (Anum), the sky-god. Below are its first three words.

<p class="cuneiform">𒄿𒉡𒈠 𒀭𒀀𒉡𒌝 𒍢𒄿𒀸</p>

```
i-nu-ma    ᵈA-nu-um              ṣi-i-rum
when       Anu(m)-NOM+mimation   exalted-NOM (agrees with Anum)
"When Anum the exalted..."
```

The sentence continues from here for dozens of lines before it finally reaches its main clause — running through "...king of the Anunnaki gods, and Enlil, lord of heaven and earth, who decrees the fates of the land, had allotted to Marduk, the firstborn son of Ea, the Enlil-functions over all mankind, made him great among the Igigi gods, and pronounced the exalted name of Babylon..." before at last arriving at "...they called me, Hammurabi, the pious prince, to cause justice to prevail in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil, that the strong might not oppress the weak..."

## Word-by-Word Analysis

| Transliteration | Cuneiform | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| i-nu-ma | 𒄿𒉡𒈠 | inu "when" + -ma (connective/emphatic particle) | when |
| ᵈA-nu-um | 𒀭𒀀𒉡𒌝 | Anu- (divine name stem) + -um (nominative case + mimation) | Anu (the sky-god) |
| ṣi-i-rum | 𒍢𒄿𒀸 | ṣīr- (adjective stem "exalted, sublime") + -um (nominative ending) | exalted, sublime |

## Grammatical Notes

- **`inūma`**: A conjunction meaning "when," marking a subordinate clause. The entire prologue opens with a single vast temporal clause introduced by this word, only reaching its main verb (roughly "I established...") dozens of lines later.
- **Case ending and mimation**: Both `Anum` and `ṣīrum` end in `-um`. This is the nominative singular case ending `-um` as found in Old Babylonian, which includes an added final nasal `-m` — mimation — that was later dropped (with accompanying vowel shortening) in subsequent dialects. See [Noun Case and Mimation](/en/akkadian/grammar/noun-case-and-mimation/).
- **Adjective agreement**: `ṣīrum`, "exalted," modifies the noun `Anum` and agrees with it in gender, number, and case, hence sharing the same `-um` ending. See [Adjective Declension and Agreement](/en/akkadian/grammar/adjective-declension-agreement/).
- **The determinative `ᵈ`**: Placed before divine names (ultimately derived from the cuneiform sign AN), this determinative is a silent semantic marker, not itself pronounced; it is conventionally transliterated as a superscript `d` (an abbreviation of DINGIR).
