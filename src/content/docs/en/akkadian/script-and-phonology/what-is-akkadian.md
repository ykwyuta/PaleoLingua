## Akkadian at a Glance

Akkadian (native *akkadûm*) was spoken in ancient Mesopotamia (roughly modern Iraq) and is one of the **earliest languages attested in written form**, recorded in cuneiform script. It is the earliest-attested member of the Semitic language family and eventually displaced Sumerian as the principal written language of the ancient Near East.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Region spoken | Mesopotamia (modern Iraq and northeastern Syria), split between southern Babylonia and northern Assyria |
| Attested period | c. 2350 BCE – c. 1st century CE (the latest texts are astronomical works, after which cuneiform use lapses) |
| Family | Afro-Asiatic > Semitic > East Semitic branch (distinct from the West Semitic branch of Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, etc.) |
| Script | Cuneiform [borrowed from Sumerian](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/cuneiform-akkadian-writing-system/) — a syllabary plus Sumerograms and determinatives |
| Basic word order | SOV (subject–object–verb) — unusual for a Semitic language, generally attributed to prolonged contact with Sumerian |
| Dialects/periods | Two broad dialect groups (Babylonian and Assyrian), each with its own internal periodization ([see the dedicated article](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/akkadian-dialects-and-periods/)) |
| Discovery & decipherment | 19th-century decipherment, led by Henry Rawlinson's work on the trilingual Behistun Inscription (Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian) |

## Akkadian's Place in the Semitic Family

Akkadian belongs to the **Semitic language family**, alongside Arabic, Hebrew, and others, but forms its own **East Semitic** branch (the only other well-attested East Semitic language is Eblaite, known from the site of Ebla in Syria). Akkadian is thought to have split from the **West Semitic** languages — Canaanite (including Hebrew and Phoenician), Aramaic, Arabic, and others — long before writing began.

Several features set Akkadian apart from its West Semitic relatives:

- Its basic word order is **SOV** (verb-final). Most West Semitic languages are basically VSO (verb–subject–object); Akkadian's shift toward verb-final order is generally attributed to centuries of contact with Sumerian, which was itself agglutinative and verb-final.
- Its noun case system shows **mimation**, a final *-m* (e.g. nominative *šarrum*, genitive *šarrim*, accusative *šarram*).
- It is written in cuneiform, a script not originally designed for a Semitic language's phonology (see [The Cuneiform Writing System for Akkadian](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/cuneiform-akkadian-writing-system/) for details).

## Why Akkadian Matters

Akkadian cuneiform documents survive in an enormous quantity and range of genres, unmatched by almost any other ancient language:

- **Law**: the Code of Hammurabi and other law collections, court records, and legal contracts.
- **Myth and literature**: narrative works such as the *Epic of Gilgamesh* and the creation epic *Enūma Eliš*.
- **Letters**: the Amarna letters (diplomatic correspondence between Egypt and its Near Eastern neighbors) and Old Assyrian merchant letters, which vividly document international relations and commerce.
- **Administrative and economic texts**: temple and palace accounts, contracts, and decrees documenting the texture of daily life.

By the second millennium BCE, Akkadian had also become the **lingua franca of diplomacy** across the eastern Mediterranean world — even the kings of Egypt and Hatti corresponded with each other in Akkadian. Learning Akkadian therefore opens a door onto nearly every aspect of ancient Near Eastern political, economic, religious, and literary life.

## Discovery and Decipherment

The decipherment of Akkadian (Babylonian and Assyrian cuneiform) is one of the great achievements of 19th-century philology, and it proceeded hand in hand with the decipherment of Old Persian. On a cliff face at Behistun in western Iran, the Achaemenid king Darius I had his res gestae carved in three languages — **Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian (Akkadian)**. The British officer and scholar Henry Rawlinson famously copied this trilingual inscription at considerable personal risk, and it became the key to decipherment. Once the relatively transparent Old Persian text had been worked out, scholars including the Irish clergyman Edward Hincks, the French scholar Jules Oppert, and Rawlinson himself used it as a lever to crack Babylonian cuneiform through the 1850s. In 1857, the Royal Asiatic Society staged a public test in which four scholars independently translated a newly discovered inscription of the Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser I; their translations agreed closely enough to convince skeptics that Akkadian decipherment was sound.

## Where the Name "Akkadian" Comes From

The name "Akkadian" derives from **Akkad (Agade)**, the capital city founded by Sargon of Akkad around the 24th century BCE. Speakers of the language called it *akkadûm*, "(the language) of Akkad," distinguishing it from Sumerian (*šumerûm*, or in Sumerian itself *eme-ĝir₁₅*).
