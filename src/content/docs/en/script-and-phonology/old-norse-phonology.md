## The vowel system

Old Norse has nine short and nine long vowels, an unusually rich vowel inventory even among the Germanic languages of its time. Length is independently contrastive for each vowel quality (see [Old Norse Transliteration](/en/script-and-phonology/old-norse-transliteration/)).

| Short | Long | Approximate tongue position |
|---|---|---|
| a | á | back, unrounded |
| e | é | front, unrounded, mid |
| i | í | front, unrounded, close |
| o | ó | back, rounded, mid |
| u | ú | back, rounded, close |
| y | ý | front, rounded, close |
| æ | — | front, unrounded, open-ish (only occurs long) |
| œ | — | front, rounded, open-ish (only occurs long) |
| ǫ | — | back-ish, rounded, open-ish (only occurs short) |

There are also three diphthongs: *ei, ey, au* (e.g., *steinn* "stone," *heyra* "hear," *auga* "eye").

## The consonant system

The consonant inventory is broadly familiar to English speakers, with a few notable features.

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| þ / ð contrast | separate letters distinguish voiceless and voiced dental fricatives (see the transliteration article for detail) |
| voiceless nasals/liquids | word-initial clusters with h, such as *hl-, hn-, hr-* (e.g., *hringr* "ring") |
| long (geminate) consonants | doubled consonant letters mark a lengthened consonant (e.g., the *ll* in *kalla* "to call") |
| final -r variation | the nominative ending *-r* can assimilate depending on the preceding consonant |

## i-umlaut

**i-umlaut** is a fronting/raising shift in a preceding vowel triggered by an *i* or *j* in the following syllable — the same kind of process comparative linguists call "vowel mutation." It took place at the Proto-Germanic stage, but its effects survive throughout Old Norse noun plurals and verb person inflections.

```
gestr (guest, sg.)  →  gestir (guests, pl.)
    e reflects the outcome of i-umlaut
```

A more striking example is the pair *fótr* ("foot") / *fœtr* ("feet"), where ó has shifted to œ. This alternation is discussed further in "[Strong and Weak Nouns](/en/grammar/strong-weak-nouns/)."

## u-umlaut

**u-umlaut** is the rounding of a preceding *a* to *ǫ*, triggered by a *u* in the following syllable.

```
land (land, sg.)  →  lǫnd (lands, pl.)
    a shifts to ǫ under u-umlaut
```

Like i-umlaut, u-umlaut shows up regularly across noun declension and verb conjugation tables, making it an important clue for memorizing paradigms.

## Stress placement

Stress in Old Norse falls, as a rule, on the **first (root) syllable of the word** — a feature shared across the Germanic languages, reflecting the shift from the mobile accent of Proto-Indo-European to a fixed, word-initial stress at the Proto-Germanic stage. No matter how many prefixes or endings are attached, the stress stays put on the root syllable.
