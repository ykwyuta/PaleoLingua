## Reading direction is not fixed

Unlike modern writing, runic inscriptions do not follow a fixed reading direction. Left-to-right is most common, but right-to-left inscriptions occur, and so does **boustrophedon** ("as the ox plows"), where direction alternates line by line. In boustrophedon texts the letters on alternating lines are often mirror-reversed, which makes them hard to read at first glance. On stones, the text sometimes winds along the edge of the carved surface, and figuring out where the inscription begins can require reading the accompanying imagery — a snake or dragon motif is a common cue.

## Word dividers

As a rough equivalent to modern word spacing, a dot (·) or a vertical stack of dots (:) sometimes marks word boundaries. This convention was never fully consistent, though, and many inscriptions run letters together with no separators at all. In those cases the reader has to infer word breaks from vocabulary knowledge and context.

```
raisti · stain · þana
```

The example above shows the formulaic phrase "raised this stone," with dots marking the word boundaries.

## Bind runes (bandrún)

Two or more runes sharing a common vertical stave can be merged into a single ligature, called a **bind rune** (Old Norse *bandrún*). This is thought to have saved carving effort or served a decorative purpose. ᛘ (m) and ᛅ (a), for instance, both have a vertical stave and can be carved sharing one stem as a single combined character. Bind runes were especially favored in carvers' signatures and wherever a lot of text had to fit into limited space.

## In practice: reading a formulaic phrase

As covered in more depth in a later reading article, Viking Age runestones repeat a standard formula: "Person A raised this stone in memory of Person B." Below is that basic memorial formula.

| Rune sequence (long-branch) | Transliteration | Word-by-word gloss |
|---|---|---|
| ᛅ ᛋᛅ ᛚᛁᛏ ᚱᛅᛁᛋᛅ ᛋᛏᛅᛁᚾ ᚦᛁᚾᛅ | *A sa lit raisa stain þina* | A ("Name") = the one who had this stone raised |

(*lit ... raisa* is a causative construction, "had [it] raised" — covered in detail in "[Viking Age Runestones](/en/old-norse/readings/viking-age-runestones/)".)

## Common pitfalls

- **No voiced/voiceless distinction**: ᛏ covers both t and d, so a spelling like *stain* should be read as equivalent to *stein* ("stone") from context.
- **Nasal dropping in spelling**: consonant clusters with a nasal are sometimes simplified in writing (e.g., *land* spelled as if *lant*).
- **Polyvalent vowel letters**: ᚢ can represent u, o, y, ø, or w, so it has to be cross-checked against known vocabulary.
- **Damage and wear**: surviving inscriptions are often partly illegible due to weathering or breakage; scholarly editions mark such gaps with brackets.
