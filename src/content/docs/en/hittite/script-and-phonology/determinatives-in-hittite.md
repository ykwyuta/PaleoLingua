## What Is a Determinative?

A **determinative** is a sign that is itself unpronounced but tells the reader what semantic category the following (occasionally preceding) word belongs to. Like most languages written in cuneiform, Hittite texts make heavy use of determinatives, shown in transliteration as superscripts. Most determinatives derive from Sumerian words and so also carry the character of Sumerograms.

## Common Determinatives

| Determinative | Origin/meaning | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| D | Sumerian DINGIR "god" | Before divine names | ᴰIŠTAR "the goddess Ishtar" |
| URU | Sumerian "city" | Before place names | ᵁᴿᵁḪattuša "the city of Ḫattuša" |
| KUR | Sumerian "mountain, land" | Before country names | ᴷᵁᴿḪatti "the land of Ḫatti" |
| ᴵ (or ᵐ) | Marker for personal names | Before male personal names | ᴵMuršili "Muršili (a royal name)" |
| MUNUS | Sumerian "woman" | Before female personal names | ᴹᵁᴺᵁˢPuduḫepa "Puduḫepa (a queen's name)" |
| GIŠ | Sumerian "wood" | Before names of wooden objects | ᴳᴵŠGU.ZA "throne (of wood)" |
| DUG | Sumerian "pot, vessel" | Before pottery/vessel names | ᴰᵁᴳḫupuwai- "a type of jar" |

## Position and Silent Function

Most determinatives are placed **before** the noun, though some, like URU (for city names), are also attested placed **after** the word (though pre-posed usage is also found, with variation by scribe and period). Unlike grammatical elements such as case endings, determinatives are not thought to have been pronounced at all. They functioned purely as a visual aid — an "annotation" or "label" for the scribe and reader.

```
ᴵmu-ur-ši-li-iš
(personal-name determinative) Muršili-NOM
"Muršili (nom.)"
```

In this example, ᴵ is simply a label marking "this is a male personal name" and would be skipped over when the word was read aloud or translated.

## Their Practical Role in the Bureaucracy

In a multilingual, document-heavy bureaucracy, determinatives helped readers quickly classify words. Personal names, divine names, and place names in particular drew on many different languages — not just native Hittite but Hurrian, Luwian, Akkadian, and more — so being able to tell at a glance "this is a god's name" or "this is a city's name" was a real convenience for scribes handling complex administrative and treaty documents.
