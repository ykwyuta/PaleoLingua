## The -a that turns a verb into a noun phrase

Sumerian has a suffix `-a` that attaches after a verb (stem plus its prefixes) and turns the whole verb phrase into a nominal unit meaning "the (thing/event) that ~." It functions much like an English relative clause, or the Japanese attributive "〜した" (as in 「建てた家」, "the house that [someone] built").

<p class="cuneiform">𒂍𒆕𒀀</p>

```
e2  du3-a
e2  du3-a
house  build-NMZ
"the built house," "the house (someone) built"
```

## Enclosing an entire verb phrase

`-a` attaches not just to the bare root but to the end of the whole verb phrase, prefixes included. That means an entire clause — subject, dative argument, and all — can be wrapped up into a single noun phrase.

<p class="cuneiform">𒈗𒂊  𒂍  𒈬𒆕𒀀</p>

```
lugal-e   e2   mu-du3-a
lugal-e   e2   mu-du3-a
king-ERG  house  build-NMZ
"the house that the king built"
```

This whole phrase, `e2 ... mu-du3-a` ("the house that the king built"), can then function as a single noun phrase within a larger sentence — for instance, taking a further case suffix such as "in ~."

## Note: a different animal from the genitive -a

As shown in [The Genitive -ak](/en/sumerian/grammar/genitive-case-ak/), the genitive suffix `-ak` can surface as `-a` depending on what follows it. This nominalizing `-a` is an entirely different morpheme in both origin and function — but because the two look identical, they are easy to confuse.

| Type of `-a` | Origin | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|---|
| Genitive `-a` | A phonological variant of `-ak` | Follows a noun phrase (e.g., lugal-la, "of the king") |
| Nominalizing `-a` | An independent suffix | Follows a verb (phrase) (e.g., du3-a, "built") |

The biggest clue for telling the two apart is whether the element immediately before `-a` is a noun phrase or a verb phrase.
