## The Chāndogya Upaniṣad and a Father-Son Dialogue

The **Upaniṣads** (*Upaniṣad*, literally "sitting near," referring to esoteric teaching received at a teacher's side) are placed at the end of the Vedic corpus and are therefore also called **Vedānta** ("the end of the Veda"). The oldest surviving layer of Upaniṣads (the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka*, the *Chāndogya*, and others) is generally dated to around 800–500 BCE. Moving away from ritual practice, they center on speculative, philosophical dialogues exploring the relationship between Brahman (the cosmic first principle) and Ātman (the essence, or self, of the individual).

Chapter 6 of the **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** contains a sequence of teachings given by the sage **Uddālaka Āruṇi** to his son **Śvetaketu**, and is one of the most important chapters for later Vedānta thought, especially Advaita ("non-dual") Vedānta. Using everyday analogies — a lump of salt dissolved in water, the tiny seed of a banyan tree — Uddālaka teaches that a single underlying reality (*sat*) grounds all existing things. The refrain that concludes each of these teachings is **"तत्त्वमसि tat tvam asi"** ("that thou art").

## The Text (Chapter 6, Section 8, excerpt)

Shown here with sandhi resolved into its component words.

```
स य एषोऽणिमैतदात्म्यमिदं सर्वम् ।
sa ya eṣo 'ṇimā, aitadātmyam idaṃ sarvam,

तत्सत्यम् । स आत्मा । तत्त्वमसि श्वेतकेतो इति ॥
tat satyam, sa ātmā, tat tvam asi śvetaketo iti ||
```

| एषः | eṣaḥ | demonstrative "this," masc. nom. sg. | this |

| ऐतदात्म्यम् | aitadātmyam | etad "this" + ātman "self," derived noun, neut. nom. sg. | having that as one's essence/self |
| इदं सर्वम् | idaṃ sarvam | idam "this" + sarva "all," neut. nom. sg. | this whole (world) |
| तत् | tat | demonstrative pronoun, neut. nom. sg. | that |

**Translation:** "That which is this subtlest essence — this whole world has that as its self. That is the real, that is the Self (Ātman). **That thou art**, O Śvetaketu."

## The Syntax of "Tat Tvam Asi"

Grammatically this is an extremely simple three-word sentence — **tat** ("that") + **tvam** ("you") + **asi** ("are") — and yet it is one of the most frequently quoted and most heavily commented-upon statements in the entire history of Indian philosophy. Here **tat** ("that") refers back to the cosmic reality or first principle discussed in the preceding context (what later terminology calls Brahman), while **tvam** ("you") refers to the individual being addressed — Śvetaketu, and by extension every learner — meaning that person's own self (Ātman). Despite its grammatical simplicity, the sentence condenses the central claim of Vedānta philosophy: that the cosmic first principle and the essence of the individual are one and the same.

Within Chapter 6, this refrain follows a series of analogies — the parable of salt dissolved in water (section 13: invisible yet present and tasteable everywhere) and the parable of the banyan seed (section 12: an invisible, subtle essence within the seed that gives rise to a great tree) — and is repeated as the closing formula nine times in total.

## Significance for Vedānta Philosophy

The 8th-century philosopher **Śaṅkara** treated this passage as one of the "great sayings" (*mahāvākyas*) and made it a central scriptural pillar of Advaita Vedānta ("non-dualism"). In Śaṅkara's reading, the statement is a direct scriptural (*śruti*) testimony that the individual self (*jīva-ātman*) and the cosmic first principle (Brahman) are essentially identical — non-dual, *advaita*. Later theistic Vedānta schools, beginning with **Rāmānuja**, did not accept this identity as a strict, complete equivalence, instead offering different accounts — for instance, a relation of part to whole, or a qualified form of identity. Interpretation of this passage continues to divide the schools of Vedānta to this day.
