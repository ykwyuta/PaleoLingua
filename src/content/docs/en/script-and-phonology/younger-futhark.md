## The standard script of the Viking Age

The Younger Futhark is the 16-rune system used across Scandinavia from roughly the 8th to the 12th century, and it accounts for the great majority of Viking Age inscriptions. Most of the several thousand surviving runestones use this system.

## Long-branch and short-twig runes

The Younger Futhark exists in two variant letterform traditions:

- **Long-branch runes**: used mainly in Denmark, common in formal contexts such as public memorial inscriptions.
- **Short-twig runes**: used mainly in Norway and Sweden, with the branches off the vertical stave simplified or dropped. Common in everyday writing, such as inscribed wooden sticks (rune-sticks).

Both traditions encode the same 16 sound values but differ in how elaborate the letterforms are. The rune "h" (*hagall*), for example, is ᚼ (a single crossbar) in the long-branch system but can appear as ᚽ in the short-twig system. In practice, the two traditions are often mixed within a single region or period.

## The cost of a smaller alphabet: ambiguity

A defining feature of the Younger Futhark is that it makes no distinction between voiced and voiceless stops. ᛏ (t), for example, can stand for either /t/ or /d/, and ᛒ (b) for either /b/ or /p/. There is also a tendency to drop the consonant before a following nasal in writing, so "land" might be spelled as if it were *lant*. Because of these gaps, reading a runic inscription requires more than phonological knowledge alone — context is essential.

## The rise of dotted runes

From the 12th century on, dots were added to existing runes to distinguish sounds more precisely, producing the so-called **dotted runes**. A dot added to ᚴ (k), for instance, gives ᚵ, dedicated to /g/; a dot added to ᛁ (i) gives ᛂ, dedicated to /e/. This development marks a step toward the medieval runic alphabet and is often attributed in part to the influence of the Latin script.
