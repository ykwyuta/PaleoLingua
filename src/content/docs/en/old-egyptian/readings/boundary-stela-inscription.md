## Background: a king's words carved at the Nubian frontier

King Senwosret III of the Middle Kingdom (12th Dynasty, reigned roughly the 1870s-1830s BCE) erected boundary stelae at Semna and Uronarti in Nubia (the region now northern Sudan). These inscriptions mark the southern limit of Egyptian control along the Nile, combining a warning against Nubians crossing the frontier except for purposes of trade with passages praising the king's own military prowess.

## Royal inscriptions as a genre

Unlike the narrative or instruction literature written on papyrus, boundary stelae are a fine example of the genre of royal or monumental inscriptions — texts carved in stone and permanently installed in a public setting. Their style is declarative and commanding, keenly aware of an audience, designed to visually and linguistically assert the king's authority and the order of the land to any passerby, present or future.

## Example: a phrase for "land" and "king"

The phrase below is a short illustrative example reflecting a theme that recurs throughout the boundary-stela genre — royal control over the land — not a verbatim quotation from the actual Semna inscriptions.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓈖𓋴𓅱 𓈖 𓏏𓄿</p>

```
nsw     n       tꜣ
king    of      land
```

*nsw n tꜣ*, "king of the land," is an illustrative phrase. The spirit running through the entire boundary-stela genre is precisely this declaration: that the king rules this land and fixes its borders.
