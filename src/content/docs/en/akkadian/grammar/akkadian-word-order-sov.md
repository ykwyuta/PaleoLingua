## Akkadian's Basic Word Order: SOV

Akkadian sentences are basically verb-final, following the order **Subject (S) – Object (O) – Verb (V)**.

<p class="cuneiform">𒀀𒉿𒈝 𒁉𒋫𒄠 𒄿𒁍𒍑</p>

```
awīlum   bītam    īpuš
man-NOM   house-ACC  he.built
"the man built the house"
```

In this sentence, the subject `awīlum` ("the man," nominative) opens the sentence, the object `bītam` ("the house," accusative) follows, and the verb `īpuš` ("he built," G-stem preterite) closes it. This subject-then-object-then-verb arrangement is the most basic word order pattern in Akkadian.

## Contrast with Other Semitic Languages: VSO Is the "Normal" Pattern Elsewhere

Akkadian's SOV order is, in fact, quite unusual within the Semitic language family as a whole. In Classical Arabic, Biblical Hebrew, and many other Semitic languages besides Akkadian, **Verb-Subject-Object (VSO)** order is the basic or at least a very common pattern (SVO also appears depending on period and register, but the verb tends to appear near the start of the clause).

| Language | Basic word order |
|---|---|
| Akkadian (East Semitic) | SOV (verb-final) |
| Classical Arabic | VSO (verb-initial) |
| Biblical Hebrew | VSO (verb-initial) |

In other words, despite belonging to the same Semitic family, Akkadian alone places its verb at the end of the sentence — a genealogically anomalous behavior.

## Why Is Akkadian Alone SOV? Contact with Sumerian

The explanation most widely accepted by scholars for this unusual word order is **prolonged language contact with Sumerian**. Sumerian, genealogically, is a language isolate entirely unrelated to the Semitic family (its genetic affiliation with any other known language has never been demonstrated), but it coexisted with Akkadian speakers in southern Mesopotamia for centuries, in close contact across administration, literature, and scholarship. Sumerian itself, as discussed at [Sumerian Word Order (SOV)](/en/sumerian/grammar/word-order-sov/), is a verb-final language.

Akkadian is believed to have inherited a VSO-type word order from Proto-Semitic, like its Semitic relatives, but over centuries of coexistence alongside the verb-final Sumerian, its syntax is thought to have been pulled toward the Sumerian pattern, eventually settling into SOV order. This is often cited in typological and language-contact studies as a striking example that syntactic structure — not merely vocabulary (including the borrowing of logograms such as [Sumerograms](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/sumerograms-in-akkadian/)) — can be reshaped through sustained language contact.
