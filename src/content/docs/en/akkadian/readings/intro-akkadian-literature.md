## The Scope of Akkadian Literature

Akkadian was written in cuneiform for more than two thousand years, from the mid-third millennium BCE (the Old Akkadian period) down to roughly the first century CE. Given this long span, and a geographic reach extending from Mesopotamia into Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant, the range of surviving Akkadian genres is remarkably broad. Law codes, royal inscriptions commemorating a king's deeds, epics and myths recounting the doings of the gods, letters for diplomacy and trade, omen texts for reading the future, proverbs and wisdom literature dispensing practical advice, and legal contracts recording everyday sales and marriages — all of these were incised on the durable medium of clay, and tens of thousands of examples survive today, often precisely because fire and ruin did not destroy them.

This article surveys the major genres covered in the readings category and points to the specific articles that treat each one in detail.

## How Genre Clusters with Period and Dialect

These genres are not evenly distributed across every period and dialect of Akkadian. Most of the great law codes and epic/mythological narratives were either composed in the Old Babylonian period (c. 2000–1600 BCE) or later recast into the literary dialect known as Standard Babylonian, transmitted to us largely through later copies — such as those from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. Practical documents like letters and contracts, by contrast, more directly reflect the everyday spoken dialect of their time and place, and some, like the Amarna letters, preserve a peripheral form of Akkadian — in that case one shaped by contact with Canaanite — that is invaluable evidence for dialect variation. Royal inscriptions reach their greatest volume and elaboration in the Neo-Assyrian period, while omen texts were systematized as a technical literature for professional diviners (bārû).

## A Taste of the Texts: The Opening Line of the Epic of Gilgamesh

A genre list alone is a bit dry, so let's look at one real piece of cuneiform. The following is the celebrated opening line (incipit) of Tablet I of the Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh (treated in full at [Excerpt from Gilgamesh](/en/akkadian/readings/gilgamesh-akkadian-excerpt/)).

<p class="cuneiform">𒊭 𒈾𒀝𒁀 𒄿𒈬𒊒</p>

```
ša           na-aq-ba      i-mu-ru
[relative]   deep-ACC      saw(3ms)-SBJV
"He who saw the Deep"
```

`ša` is an invariable relative particle meaning "who/that," unmarked for gender, number, or case. `naqba` is the accusative singular of the noun `naqbu`, "the deep, the totality of things." `īmuru` is the G-stem preterite third-person masculine singular of the verb `amāru`, "to see," with the subjunctive ending `-u` that Akkadian requires on verbs inside a relative clause introduced by `ša`. In a single line, then, we see a working example of the relative-clause construction and subordinate-clause verbal ending covered in the grammar category.
