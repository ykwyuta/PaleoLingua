## Adjectives have strong and weak forms too

Just as [Strong and Weak Nouns](/en/old-norse/grammar/strong-weak-nouns/) split into two declension classes, Old Norse adjectives also come in **strong** and **weak** varieties. Unlike nouns, though, a single adjective uses both forms depending on the meaning intended — a system closely paralleled by modern German adjective declension, where the ending changes depending on whether a definite article is present.

## The basic usage rule

| Declension | Used when |
|---|---|
| Strong | the noun has no definite article, and in predicative use (the "X" in "the man is X") |
| Weak | the noun carries a definite article (suffixed or the free-standing *hinn*), or is accompanied by a demonstrative or possessive pronoun |

## gamall ("old"), strong declension (masc. sg.)

| Case | Strong |
|---|---|
| Nominative | gamall |
| Accusative | gamlan |
| Genitive | gamals |
| Dative | gömlum |

## gamall ("old"), weak declension (masc. sg.)

| Case | Weak |
|---|---|
| Nominative | gamli |
| Accusative | gamla |
| Genitive | gamla |
| Dative | gamla |

As with weak nouns, the weak adjective largely collapses the case distinctions, with *gamla* covering three of the four cases.

## Usage contrast in example sentences

**Strong (no article, indefinite)**

```
gamall  maðr  kom
gamall  maðr  kom
old(STRONG) man(NOM) came
"An old man came."
```

**Weak (with the definite article)**

```
hinn gamli maðr  kom
hinn  gaml-i  maðr  kom
the   old-WEAK  man(NOM) came
"The old man came."
```

The same adjective, *gamall* ("old"), takes a different ending depending on whether the noun carries a definite article. This tracks a semantic distinction in **definiteness**: "an old man" (unspecified, strong) versus "the old man" (already established in discourse, weak).
