## Background: the masterpiece of Middle Egyptian narrative

*The Tale of Sinuhe* was composed early in the 12th Dynasty (early Middle Kingdom, roughly the 1900s BCE) and is widely regarded as the single greatest achievement of Middle Egyptian narrative literature. It survives not only in papyrus copies but in a large number of ostraca (inscribed potsherds and limestone flakes), which attest to its use as a widely copied school classic.

## Plot summary

Upon learning of the sudden death of King Amenemhat I, the courtier Sinuhe — for reasons the text never fully explains, seized by fear and panic rather than any documented wrongdoing — flees Egypt. He goes into exile in the region of Syria-Palestine, which the Egyptians called the land of Retjenu, where under the protection of a local ruler he prospers and achieves great success. In old age, however, homesickness overtakes him, and King Senwosret I invites him back, promising a return to Egypt and a proper burial. Sinuhe accepts, and the story closes with his homecoming.

The ending — in which success abroad is ultimately less important than being buried correctly on Egyptian soil — is often read as a vivid expression of ancient Egyptian attitudes toward death and the afterlife.

## Example: a phrase evoking "departure" and "return"

The phrase below is a short illustrative example reflecting the story's central theme of departure, not a verbatim quotation from any manuscript. Sinuhe's flight from Egypt (thematically tied to the verb *prj*, "to go forth, depart") and his eventual return following the king's letter (expressed with *jwi*, "to come") together frame the entire narrative arc.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓊪𓂋𓇋 𓅓 𓏏𓄿</p>

```
prj      m      tꜣ
go forth  from   land
```

*prj m tꜣ*, "to go forth from the land," is an illustrative phrase evoking this theme. The story of Sinuhe begins with exactly this act of leaving the land, and closes only in old age with what could be described using the verb *jwi* ("to come") — a return. Reading the tale as bracketed by this pair of basic motion verbs, "go forth" and "come," is a useful way to hold its shape in mind.
