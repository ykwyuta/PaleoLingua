## Background: New Kingdom secular verse, and a note on language stage

Ancient Egyptian love poetry survives mainly on papyri from the New Kingdom (the Late Egyptian language stage, roughly 1300-1100 BCE), with Papyrus Chester Beatty I and Papyrus Harris 500 among the best-known sources.

One point worth flagging up front: this genre is attested strictly in Late Egyptian, not Middle Egyptian proper (see the language-stage table in [What Is Old Egyptian?](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/what-is-old-egyptian/)). Late Egyptian differs from Middle Egyptian in several grammatical respects, the rise of a definite article being one of the most notable. That said, its vocabulary and basic structure remain close enough to Middle Egyptian that anyone trained in the earlier stage can read it without major difficulty.

## Content: gardens, birds, the Nile, and "brother"/"sister"

Love poems are frequently voiced by a young man or woman addressing the beloved as "brother" (sn) or "sister" (snt). This is not a literal statement of blood kinship but a conventional term of endearment used between lovers. The poems are rich in natural imagery — gardens, small birds, the Nile — and lovesickness is often described almost as if it were a genuine physical illness.

## Example: the "brother"/"sister" address

The phrase below is a short illustrative example reflecting the conventional "brother"/"sister" address common in love poetry, not a quotation from any specific poem.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓋴𓈖𓏏 𓈖𓆑𓂋𓏏</p>

```
snt      nfrt
sister   beautiful (feminine)
```

*snt* is spelled purely with the uniliteral signs s (𓋴), n (𓈖), and t (𓏏), meaning "sister" as a conventional address to a lover (the masculine counterpart is *sn*, spelled s + n). *nfrt* is *nfr* ("good, beautiful") with the feminine ending *-t* added. *snt nfrt*, "beautiful sister" (i.e., "beautiful beloved"), is the illustrative phrase.
