[An Overview of Sanskrit Noun Declension](/en/sanskrit/grammar/noun-declension-overview/) listed the eight cases; this article digs into what role each case actually plays in real sentences, with worked examples. Note that a single case can carry more than one function, and that the functions of different cases sometimes partially overlap.

## Nominative: Subject of the Sentence

The nominative marks the **subject** of a sentence — the most basic function of any case.

```
बालकः क्रीडति।
bālakaḥ krīḍati.
boy-NOM play-3SG.PRES
"The boy plays."
```

## Vocative: Direct Address

The vocative is used to **address** an interlocutor directly. It stands syntactically apart from the rest of the sentence.

```
हे राम, अत्र आगच्छ।
he rāma, atra āgaccha.
oh Rāma-VOC here come-2SG.IMP
"O Rāma, come here."
```

## Accusative: Direct Object and Goal of Motion

The accusative's core function is marking the **direct object** of a transitive verb, but it also expresses the **goal of motion** ("to, toward").

```
सः ग्रामं गच्छति।
saḥ grāmaṃ gacchati.
he-NOM village-ACC go-3SG.PRES
"He goes to the village." (goal of motion)
```

## Instrumental: Means, Instrument, Agent

The instrumental expresses "by/with," covering both **means/instrument** and, in passive constructions, the **agent** (the semantic subject).

```
सः हस्तेन लिखति।
saḥ hastena likhati.
he-NOM hand-INS write-3SG.PRES
"He writes with his hand." (means)
```

```
रामेण पत्रं लिख्यते।
rāmeṇa patraṃ likhyate.
Rāma-INS letter-NOM write-3SG.PRES.PASS
"The letter is written by Rāma." (agent of a passive)
```

Note that the subject of an active sentence shifts to the instrumental in the corresponding passive — a mirror image of how the object of an active sentence becomes the nominative subject in the passive.

## Dative: Indirect Object and Purpose

The dative expresses the **indirect object** ("to") as well as **purpose/beneficiary** ("for the sake of").

```
गुरुः शिष्याय पुस्तकं ददाति।
guruḥ śiṣyāya pustakaṃ dadāti.
teacher-NOM student-DAT book-ACC give-3SG.PRES
"The teacher gives a book to the student." (indirect object)
```

```
सः जलाय गच्छति।
saḥ jalāya gacchati.
he-NOM water-DAT go-3SG.PRES
"He goes for water." (purpose)
```

## Ablative: Point of Separation and Standard of Comparison

The ablative's most basic function is marking the **point of separation** ("from"), but it has a distinctive additional use marking the **standard of comparison** ("than") — Sanskrit has no comparative case ending of its own, so this ablative usage is the standard way to build comparative constructions.

```
वृक्षात् पत्रं पतति।
vṛkṣāt patraṃ patati.
tree-ABL leaf-NOM fall-3SG.PRES
"A leaf falls from the tree." (point of separation)
```

```
रामः लक्ष्मणात् बलवत्तरः।
rāmaḥ lakṣmaṇāt balavattaraḥ.
Rāma-NOM Lakṣmaṇa-ABL stronger-NOM
"Rāma is stronger than Lakṣmaṇa." (standard of comparison)
```

## Genitive: Possession and Belonging

The genitive expresses "of" — **possession or belonging**. Unlike other cases, which are typically governed directly by the verb, the genitive normally modifies another noun within a noun phrase.

```
रामस्य पुत्रः वनं गच्छति।
rāmasya putraḥ vanaṃ gacchati.
Rāma-GEN son-NOM forest-ACC go-3SG.PRES
"Rāma's son goes to the forest."
```

### Overlap Between Genitive and Dative

With verbs of giving or offering, the recipient is occasionally marked with the genitive in the textual tradition, overlapping in meaning with the dative. As a rule of thumb, though, a recipient governed directly by the verb takes the dative, while a possessive relation modifying a noun takes the genitive.

## Locative: Place, Time, and Circumstance (Including the Locative Absolute)

The locative expresses **location** ("in/at") and **time** ("when"), and is also used in the **locative absolute** construction, an independent adverbial clause of circumstance.

```
गृहे बालकः तिष्ठति।
gṛhe bālakaḥ tiṣṭhati.
house-LOC boy-NOM stand-3SG.PRES
"The boy is standing in the house." (location)
```

```
सूर्ये उदिते बालकाः क्रीडन्ति।
sūrye udite bālakāḥ krīḍanti.
sun-LOC risen-LOC boy.PL-NOM play-3PL.PRES
"When the sun has risen, the boys play." (locative absolute: an adverbial clause with its own subject)
```

The locative absolute pairs a noun in the locative with an agreeing participle (here, udite "having risen") to form an adverbial subordinate clause with a subject distinct from that of the main clause. This construction is also discussed in [Participles in Sanskrit](/en/sanskrit/grammar/participles-in-sanskrit/).
