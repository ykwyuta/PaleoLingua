## Two ways to write a number

Akkadian tablets record quantities in two fundamentally different ways. One is a set of **dedicated numeral signs** — wedges and "Winkelhaken" (angled, claw-shaped impressions) inherited directly from Sumerian accounting practice — that indicate a quantity directly. The other is to spell out the actual Akkadian **number word** that would be spoken, syllable by syllable. The two serve different purposes: administrative and economic records favor the former, while contexts where a number functions as an inflected word in a sentence — a subject, an object, agreeing in gender with its noun — call for the latter.

## Dedicated numeral signs: a Sumerian sexagesimal inheritance

Akkadian's dedicated numeral signs are a direct continuation of the sexagesimal (base-60) positional system used in Sumerian administrative records. Three basic signs anchor the system.

| Number | Cuneiform | Sign name | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 𒁹 | DIŠ | A single vertical wedge |
| 10 | 𒌋 | U | A single Winkelhaken (claw-shaped impression) |
| 60 | 𒁹 | DIŠ (large) | The exact same shape as 1, impressed at a larger size |

Numbers 1 through 9 were shown by repeating the DIŠ sign the required number of times (later scribal tradition also developed dedicated ligatures bundling several wedges together, e.g. for 3), and multiples of 10 by repeating the U sign. Because the system is base-60, "60" functions as a new starting point equivalent to "1" one level up — and the older Sumerian numeral system exploited this elegantly, representing 60 with the exact same wedge shape as 1, simply impressed larger. This system passed unchanged into Akkadian administrative and economic documents and remained the standard way of recording quantities on clay tablets.

## Syllabic number words: numbers as spoken Akkadian

A dedicated numeral sign only indicates a quantity directly — it carries no information about how the number was actually pronounced in Akkadian. When a number appeared in running text functioning as an inflected word — a subject or object, agreeing in gender and case — scribes instead spelled out the native Akkadian number word syllabically. Take `ištēn` "one":

<p class="cuneiform">𒅖𒋼𒂗</p>

```
iš-te-en
ištēn "one" (masculine cardinal number)
```

The same is true of `šina` "two":

<p class="cuneiform">𒅆𒈾</p>

```
ši-na
šina "two" (masculine cardinal number)
```

And `šalāš` "three" can likewise be spelled syllabically:

<p class="cuneiform">𒊭𒆷𒀸</p>

```
ša-la-aš
šalāš "three" (feminine cardinal number)
```

Note that Akkadian cardinal numbers show an unusual gender-agreement pattern when modifying a noun — for most numbers, a masculine noun triggers a feminine-form number word and vice versa, a phenomenon called "polarity" or "chiastic concord." This grammatical behavior is covered in more detail in the grammar section of this site.

## The legacy of the sexagesimal system

Why base 60 rather than base 10? The system is generally traced back to Sumerian accounting practice. One commonly cited reason is that 60 has an unusually large number of divisors (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30), which made it convenient for fraction arithmetic and for dividing up land, grain, and other resources. The sexagesimal system long outlived spoken Sumerian itself, remaining in continuous use in Akkadian administrative, astronomical, and mathematical texts for centuries. Even today, the fact that an hour is divided into 60 minutes, and a minute into 60 seconds, is often cited as a distant echo of this system.
