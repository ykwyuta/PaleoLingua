## A single sign can be read in more than one way

The first thing to grasp about reading cuneiform is that a single sign can be read differently depending on context. The same sign's reading — and meaning — changes entirely depending on which of three roles it is playing:

1. **As a logogram** — the sign stands for an entire word.
2. **As a syllabogram** — the sign borrows only the *sound* of that word to spell part of some other word.
3. **As a determinative** — the sign is silent, marking only the semantic category of the word next to it.

## A case study: the three faces of "𒀭"

Take the sign 𒀭, used for the sky-god An, and look at all three roles in turn.

```
𒀭
an
アン
(logogram) sky, the sky-god An
```

Used alone like this, the sign can be read as the logogram for "sky" or for the god An himself.

```
𒀭
dingir
ディンギル
(logogram) god (the common noun "god")
```

The very same sign can also be read as the logogram for a different word entirely, the common noun `dingir` ("god"). This phenomenon — one sign standing for more than one word — connects to the topics covered in [Cuneiform as a Syllabary](/en/sumerian/script-and-phonology/cuneiform-syllabary/) and [Logograms and Determinatives](/en/sumerian/script-and-phonology/logograms-and-determinatives/).

```
𒀭𒋀𒆠
d nanna
ナンナ
(determinative + proper noun) the moon-god Nanna
```

The same sign is also used as the determinative `d`, placed before divine names, and in that role it is never pronounced at all.

## Clues for telling the roles apart

So how does a reader, faced with an actual tablet, decide which "face" a sign is wearing? The main clues are:

| Clue | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Position | If the sign sits directly before (or after) a noun and doesn't function as an independent word on its own, it is probably a determinative |
| Context | The surrounding words tell you whether the sign stands for a whole word or spells part of another word's sound |
| Familiarity with genre formulas | Recognizing the stock phrasing of a genre — royal inscriptions, administrative records, and so on — narrows down the likely reading |

## Summary

Reading cuneiform is not a mechanical, one-to-one lookup between sign and sound, the way flipping through a dictionary might be. It's an ongoing act of contextual judgment: deciding, sign by sign, whether it is currently functioning as a logogram, a syllabogram, or a determinative. Getting comfortable with that judgment call is the first real step toward reading cuneiform.
