## Background: wonder-tales told to King Khufu

The Westcar Papyrus (Papyrus Westcar) is a collection of stories about wondrous feats performed by magicians, framed as a series of tales told one after another to King Khufu (Cheops), builder of the Great Pyramid, by his own sons. The surviving manuscript itself is generally dated to the late Middle Kingdom or early Second Intermediate Period, though the individual tales it contains may be considerably older. The papyrus is now held in the Egyptian Museum in Berlin.

## Contents

The collection includes several marvel-tales, among them the famous story of a magician who reattaches the severed head of a decapitated animal through magic. Even better known is the tale explaining the miraculous birth of the first three kings of the 5th Dynasty: they are born through a divine union in which the sun-god Ra is their true father. This episode is frequently cited in Egyptology as key evidence for the ideological link between kingship and the sun-god Ra.

## Example: a phrase for "son of a god"

The phrase below is a short illustrative example reflecting the story's central theme of divine sonship, not a verbatim quotation from the papyrus text. In Egyptian, the most basic way to express "the X of Y" is the direct genitive — simply placing two nouns side by side — and *zꜣ nṯr* ("son of god") is a classic instance of that pattern.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓊃𓄿 𓊹</p>

```
zꜣ      nṯr
son     god
```

*zꜣ nṯr* means "son of a god." The latter half of the Westcar Papyrus tells precisely this — the birth story of kings born as "sons of the god" (the sun-god Ra) — and this short phrase captures the core of that theme.
