## Quick facts

Sumerian (native name *eme-ĝir15*, often translated "the noble/royal tongue") was spoken in southern Mesopotamia (roughly modern southern Iraq) during the 3rd millennium BCE. It is among the very first languages ever committed to writing, and cuneiform script is generally thought to have been developed specifically to record it.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Region spoken | Southern Mesopotamia (Sumer, modern southern Iraq) |
| Spoken as a vernacular | 3rd millennium BCE to roughly 2000 BCE |
| Used as a written/learned language | until roughly the 1st century CE |
| Genetic affiliation | Language isolate (no established relatives) |
| Script | Cuneiform (mixed logographic and syllabic) |

## A language isolate

Sumerian is not related to Akkadian, the Semitic language that came to be spoken alongside (and eventually replaced) it in Mesopotamia. Despite various proposals linking Sumerian to Elamite or to the Northeast Caucasian languages, no genetic relationship to any other known language or language family has been established. Sumerian remains classified as a language isolate.

## How long it lasted

By around 2000 BCE, Sumerian had largely been supplanted by Akkadian as an everyday spoken language in southern Mesopotamia. For roughly two more millennia, however, it continued to serve — much as Latin did in medieval Europe — as a learned, liturgical, and scribal-school language. Sumerian hymns and lexical texts were still being copied on some of the very last cuneiform tablets, produced around the 1st century CE.

## The surviving corpus

Several hundred thousand cuneiform tablets bearing Sumerian are known today. The overwhelming majority are administrative and economic records (accounts, contracts, receipts), but the corpus also includes royal inscriptions, legal texts, and a substantial body of literature: portions of the Epic of Gilgamesh, royal hymns, laments, and proverb collections.
