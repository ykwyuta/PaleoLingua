## Two verb stems: Ḫamṭu and Marû

Rather than tense (past/present/future) as such, Sumerian verbs are built around an aspectual opposition between two stems — traditionally labeled, using terms borrowed from Akkadian, "ḫamṭu" ("quick") and "marû" ("fat," i.e., slow/drawn-out).

| Stem | Rough meaning | Typical translation |
|---|---|---|
| Ḫamṭu | a completed, single event | "did X" (past-like) |
| Marû | an ongoing, repeated, or durative event | "is doing X" / "will do X" (present/future-like) |

## An example of stem suppletion: "to go"

For most verbs, the ḫamṭu and marû forms differ only in their affixes. But a handful of very common basic verbs use entirely different roots for the two stems — a pattern called suppletion. The classic example is "to go."

| Aspect | Stem | Cuneiform |
|---|---|---|
| Ḫamṭu ("went") | ĝen | 𒁺 |
| Marû ("goes/is going") | du | 𒁺 |

`ĝen` and `du` are distinct roots by descent and by their Latin spelling — yet, intriguingly, real cuneiform texts write both with the very same DU sign 𒁺, leaving context to signal which reading is meant.

<p class="cuneiform">𒂍𒂠   𒄿𒁺</p>

```
e2-še3   i3-ĝen
house-TERM he.went
"He went to the house." (ḫamṭu stem)
```

## Why this distinction matters

The ḫamṭu/marû distinction affects how person markers and prefixes attach to the verb, making it foundational to the whole Sumerian verbal system. At this introductory stage, it's enough to grasp the core fact: Sumerian verbs come in two aspectual "shapes" — a completed (ḫamṭu) one and an ongoing (marû) one — and for some common verbs, these shapes use entirely different roots.
