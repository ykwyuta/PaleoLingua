## The Middle Kingdom: Egypt's "classical age"

The Middle Kingdom (roughly 2100-1600 BCE) is often called the "classical age" of Egyptian literature. Works composed in this period went on to be studied and copied as normative models of fine writing throughout the New Kingdom and beyond; Egyptologists frequently compare this to the position Latin classics held in later European education (see also [What Is Old Egyptian?](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/what-is-old-egyptian/)).

This article doubles as a table of contents for the Readings & Examples category. Below, we survey the four major genres of Middle Egyptian literature and link to articles on their representative works.

## Four major genres

| Genre | Characteristics | Representative works |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative / adventure tales | First- or third-person stories of adventure abroad or marvelous events | *The Tale of Sinuhe*, *The Shipwrecked Sailor*, the Westcar Papyrus |
| Instruction literature (Sebayt) | Didactic texts framed as an elder's (often a high official's) advice to his son | *The Instruction of Ptahhotep*, *The Instruction for Merikare* |
| Religious / funerary literature | Spells intended to secure the well-being of the king or an individual in the afterlife | The *Pyramid Texts* (originating in the Old Egyptian stage) and their Middle Kingdom successor, the *Coffin Texts* |
| Laments / pessimistic literature | A more unusual, reflective body of works on social collapse and personal anguish | *The Admonitions of Ipuwer*, *The Dialogue of a Man with His Ba* |

Royal hymns celebrating a king's deeds and practical or commemorative inscriptions (tomb autobiographies, boundary stelae, and the like) were also composed in Middle Egyptian and form an important body of evidence in their own right.

## Middle Egyptian's afterlife as a classical language

Even after Middle Egyptian ceased to be the spoken vernacular at the end of the Middle Kingdom, it continued to be copied as a school curriculum text for roughly 1,500 years. Tellingly, most surviving manuscripts of *The Tale of Sinuhe* are actually New Kingdom copies made centuries after the story was composed — vivid evidence of Middle Egyptian's status as a living classical language.

## Example: what it means to read this literature

The short phrase below is illustrative of an ideal that recurs throughout Middle Egyptian literature, not a verbatim quotation from any single work. Egyptian scribes held "beautiful speech" (mdw nfr) up as an ideal, and the phrase can stand as something of a keyword for the entire literary tradition.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓅓𓂧𓅱 𓈖𓆑𓂋</p>

```
mdw     nfr
word    good (beautiful, perfect)
```

Combining `mdw` ("word, speech") with `nfr` ("good, beautiful, perfect"), *mdw nfr* means "beautiful speech." One might say that crafting exactly this — beautiful speech — was one of the central ideals of Egyptian literature.
