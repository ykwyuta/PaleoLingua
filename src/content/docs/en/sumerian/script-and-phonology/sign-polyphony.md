## The flip side of homophony

[Cuneiform as a Syllabary](/en/sumerian/script-and-phonology/cuneiform-syllabary/) covered "homophony" — several different signs sharing the same sound, distinguished by index numbers. This article looks at the mirror-image phenomenon: "polyphony," where a single sign carries several different sounds/readings depending on context.

## A case study: the many readings of the DU sign

The sign DU, frequently used in verbs of motion and arrival, is one of the best-known examples of a highly polyphonic sign in Sumerian.

```
𒁺
du
ドゥ
(reading 1) to go, to come (one of the marû-stem forms)
```

```
𒁺
gin
ギン
(reading 2) to go (a close meaning, but used in a different grammatical context)
```

```
𒁺
tum2
トゥム
(reading 3) to take away, to carry
```

```
𒁺
de6
デ
(reading 4) to bring, to carry hither (a ventive-leaning context)
```

Despite being the exact same glyph, 𒁺, these four readings differ from one another both in sound and in shades of meaning. Which reading applies is determined by context — which grammatical form is in use, what prefixes surround it, and so on.

## Why did one sign end up with multiple readings?

Polyphony is generally thought to have arisen because several originally distinct pictographs, representing different words, happened to converge on the same shape as the script simplified and consolidated over time. In other words, what looks like a single sign today can, traced back far enough, turn out to be the merger of several unrelated "ancestor" signs.

## How it differs from homophony

| Phenomenon | Direction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Homophony | Multiple signs → same sound | `du`, `du3`, `du6` (all pronounced "du") |
| Polyphony | One sign → multiple sounds | 𒁺 (`du`, `gin`, `tum2`, `de6`) |

These two phenomena point in opposite directions, but both illustrate the same underlying truth about cuneiform: a sign and a sound are not necessarily in a one-to-one relationship.
