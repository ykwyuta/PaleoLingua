## Background: the "democratization" of afterlife literature

The Coffin Texts are funerary spells of the Middle Kingdom (roughly 2100-1600 BCE) written on the interior surfaces of wooden coffins. Unlike the Pyramid Texts, carved into the stone interior walls of royal pyramids (see [The Pyramid Texts: An Excerpt](/en/old-egyptian/readings/pyramid-texts-excerpt/)), the owners of these coffins are frequently not kings at all but regional elites and officials. This shift is often described as a "democratization" of afterlife literature — the privilege of eternal life after death, once reserved for the king alone, extended to a much broader stratum of society.

Many Coffin Texts spells derive from, or adapt, the Pyramid Texts tradition, while a great deal of entirely new material was also added. Some of this new material would, in turn, become the precursor of the New Kingdom's Book of the Dead (see [The Book of the Dead, Spell 125: The Weighing of the Heart](/en/old-egyptian/readings/book-of-the-dead-spell125/)).

## Example: the ba and the ka — two facets of the soul

The phrase below is a short illustrative example reflecting one of the Coffin Texts' central themes — the soul's mode of existence in the afterlife — not a verbatim quotation from any specific spell. In Egyptian belief, a person possessed two distinct spiritual components: the *ba* (bꜣ), symbolizing individual personality and mobility (often depicted as a human-headed bird), and the *ka* (kꜣ), a life-force that had to be sustained through offerings.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓃀𓄿 𓎡𓄿</p>

```
bꜣ      kꜣ
ba       ka
```

*bꜣ kꜣ*, "the ba and the ka," is an illustrative phrase pairing the two spiritual components that make up the deceased. Many Coffin Texts spells aim to ensure that a deceased person's ba and ka remain safely joined and functioning in the afterlife.
