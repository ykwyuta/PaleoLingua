## What transliteration is

"Transliteration" means replacing each cuneiform sign with a roman-alphabet representation of the sound or word it stands for. When scholars read an actual tablet, they first produce a transliteration, then move on to grammatical analysis and translation. Every example on this site is likewise presented in transliterated form.

## The basic conventions

| Convention | What it marks | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Italics | Words spelled syllabically are set in italics (this site uses plain monospace text instead) | *lugal* |
| Hyphen (-) | Syllable breaks within a single word | du-mu ("child") |
| Period (.) | A boundary between logograms combined and read as a single word | dub.sar ("scribe," literally "tablet-writer") |
| Superscript | Silent determinatives (unpronounced classifiers) | `d`nanna, uruki |
| Subscript index number | Distinguishes different signs that share the same sound | du, du3, du6 |
| Capital letters (Sumerograms) | Used for Sumerian words embedded in Akkadian text, or when a sign's intended reading is uncertain | LUGAL |

## Marking damage, restoration, and uncertainty

Tablets are frequently damaged, and transliteration includes notation to show exactly what state the text is in.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `[ ]` (square brackets) | Text is broken away; the enclosed reading is restored from context |
| `⌈ ⌉` (half brackets) | Only part of the sign survives; the reading is not fully certain |
| `?` | The reading or interpretation is uncertain |
| `x` | A sign is present but cannot be identified |

## Where this fits in your learning

These conventions exist purely so scholars can communicate a tablet's physical condition and the certainty of a reading — they are separate from Sumerian grammar itself. To keep the examples in this course readable, brackets and damage markers are generally omitted, and only well-established readings are given.
