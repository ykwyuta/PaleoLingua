## Hieroglyphs are not a "phonetic alphabet"

When people picture hieroglyphs, they often imagine a set of pictures that each simply stand for a sound, like a picture-based alphabet. In reality, hieroglyphic writing is a much more layered system, combining three fundamentally different kinds of signs — **logograms** (word-signs), **phonograms** (sound-signs), and **determinatives** (silent semantic classifiers) — often within the very same word.

## Three kinds of signs

| Type | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Logogram | A single sign stands for an entire word (its meaning and roughly its sound) | 𓉐 (a house floor plan) = pr, "house" |
| Phonogram | Represents sound (a sequence of consonants) only, unrelated to meaning. Subdivided into uniliteral, biliteral, and triliteral signs | 𓅓 = m (uniliteral), 𓄤 = nfr (biliteral) |
| Determinative | Unpronounced sign that marks which semantic category the preceding word belongs to | 𓂻 (walking legs) marks verbs of motion |

Phonograms are further divided into **uniliteral signs**, each representing a single consonant (the 24-sign set covered in the next article), **biliteral signs**, each representing a sequence of two consonants, and **triliteral signs**, each representing a sequence of three. These categories are explored in detail in "[The 24 Uniliteral Signs](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/uniliteral-signs-alphabet/)" and "[Biliteral and Triliteral Signs](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/biliteral-triliteral-signs/)".

## A worked example: how the pieces combine in one word

Egyptian words are frequently spelled with a combination of "phonograms that spell the sound" plus "a determinative that fixes the meaning." Take nfr, "good, beautiful," which can be written with a single biliteral sign (𓄤) for the sound, followed by a determinative marking abstract concepts (𓏛, a papyrus roll).

<p class="hieroglyph">𓄤𓏛</p>

```
nfr
good, beautiful, perfect (biliteral sign + abstract-concept determinative)
```

The same sound can also be spelled purely phonetically, using three uniliteral signs.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓈖𓆑𓂋</p>

```
nfr
good, beautiful, perfect (fully phonetic spelling with three uniliteral signs)
```

The coexistence of multiple valid spellings for the same word — a lone logogram, a purely phonetic spelling, or a phonetic spelling plus determinative — is one of the defining features of the hieroglyphic system.

## Why no vowels?

Egyptian phonograms represent **consonants (plus the semi-vowels j and w) only** — vowels are, as a rule, not written at all. This is generally attributed to Egyptian's membership in the Afroasiatic language family (alongside Semitic and others), where a word's core meaning is carried by its consonantal root. Once the reader recognized the root, context and native fluency supplied the appropriate vowels — much as traditional Arabic or Hebrew orthography can omit vowel marks. Hieroglyphic writing, then, is best understood not as a picture-script or a classical alphabet, but as a **consonant-centered system that blends logographic and phonographic principles**.
