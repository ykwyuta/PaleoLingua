## Stylus and clay

Cuneiform was written by pressing a reed stylus with an obliquely cut tip into a damp clay tablet. Rather than "drawing" a line with a brush or pen, the scribe "impressed" the corner or edge of the stylus into the clay, leaving a wedge-shaped (triangular in cross-section) mark. Every one of cuneiform's several hundred signs is built up entirely out of combinations of this one basic motion.

## The basic wedge types

| Wedge type | How it's made |
|---|---|
| Horizontal wedge | Stylus pressed in horizontally |
| Vertical wedge | Stylus pressed in vertically |
| Oblique wedge | Stylus pressed in at a diagonal |
| Winkelhaken ("angle hook") | Stylus corner jabbed straight down, making a small angular mark — used, for example, in the numeral "10" |

## The 90-degree rotation

In its earliest phase (through the first part of the 3rd millennium BCE), cuneiform was written in columns running top to bottom, with the columns themselves arranged right to left. Sometime around 2600 BCE, the entire system rotated 90 degrees counterclockwise, giving rise to the arrangement familiar today: signs running left to right, in rows arranged top to bottom. This shift is generally attributed to scribes finding it more efficient to hold and write on the tablet turned on its side.

As a side effect of this rotation, the early pictographic signs — originally oriented upright — appear "lying on their side" in all later cuneiform. A sign that began as an upright drawing of an ox's head, for instance, shows up rotated 90 degrees in later script forms.

## What this means for learners

Every transliteration a student or researcher encounters today reflects this post-rotation layout: left to right, top to bottom. That means every example and table in this course can simply be read left to right, exactly like ordinary alphabetic text.
