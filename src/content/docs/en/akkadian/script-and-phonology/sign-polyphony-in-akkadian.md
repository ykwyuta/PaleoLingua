## One sign, many readings

One feature of cuneiform that no learner can avoid is "polyphony" — the same glyph representing several different sounds or words depending on context. [Transliteration Conventions](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/transliteration-conventions/) covered `du`, `du₂`, `du₃` — several *different* signs sharing the same sound, a phenomenon called homophony. Polyphony is the mirror image of that.

## A case study: the many readings of the UD sign

The sign used to write "sun" and "day," known as UD, is one of the most heavily polyphonic signs in Akkadian cuneiform.

<p class="cuneiform">𒌓</p>

```
u4
(reading 1) a Sumerian-derived syllable "u," used in words relating to day/time
```

<p class="cuneiform">𒌓</p>

```
tu2
(reading 2) another Sumerian-derived syllable, "tu"
```

<p class="cuneiform">𒌓</p>

```
tam
(reading 3) yet another Sumerian-derived syllable, "tam"
```

<p class="cuneiform">𒌓</p>

```
utu
(reading 4) the Sumerian word/deity name for "sun" (a logographic reading)
```

<p class="cuneiform">𒌓</p>

```
UD
(Akkadian reading) Šamaš, "the sun-god, the sun" — a logographic reading within an Akkadian text
```

Despite being the exact same glyph, 𒌓, these readings differ from one another both in sound and in the language they belong to. The name of this site's mascot character, the sun-god Šamaš, comes directly from this same UD sign used as a logogram — written `UD`, read `d`UTU.

## Why did so many readings converge on one sign?

Several factors are usually cited. First, cuneiform was used continuously for centuries — sometimes over a millennium — to write two languages with very different grammar and phonology: Sumerian, a language isolate, and Akkadian, a Semitic language. Once a sign came to serve as the logogram for several near-synonymous Sumerian words (in this case, several Sumerian words touching on "day," "sun," "white/bright"), it automatically inherited all of those words' pronunciations as possible "readings." Second, sign simplification and consolidation over time sometimes caused originally distinct pictographs to converge on the same shape by pure historical accident. Third, Akkadian speakers reused Sumerian logograms for the corresponding Akkadian word's meaning as well — here, `šamšu`/Šamaš "sun" — layering a brand-new, meaning-based Akkadian reading on top of the inherited Sumerian syllabic readings.

## How does a reader tell the readings apart?

Since the same sign can carry multiple readings, a reader has only context to go on. The relevant clues include whether the sign stands alone as a full word or is combined with other signs as part of a syllabic spelling, what determinatives or phonetic complements surround it, and — perhaps most importantly — what kind of document is being read (an administrative record, a literary composition, a prayer to the gods), since that shapes which reading is likely to be intended.

It's worth noting that polyphony cannot be resolved with subscript index numbers. Index numbers are a tool for distinguishing homophony — several different signs sharing the same sound — not for disambiguating the several different sounds a single polyphonic sign can carry. Only context can do that.

## How it differs from homophony

| Phenomenon | Direction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Homophony | Multiple signs → same sound | `du` 𒁺, `du₂` 𒌅, `du₃` 𒆕 (all pronounced "du") |
| Polyphony | One sign → multiple sounds | 𒌓 (`u4`, `tu2`, `tam`, `utu`, and Akkadian `šamaš`) |

These two phenomena point in exactly opposite directions, but both illustrate the same underlying truth about the cuneiform writing system: a sign and a sound are never guaranteed to be in a one-to-one relationship.
