## What Is the Hitopadeśa?

The **Hitopadeśa** ("Beneficial Instruction") is a work of didactic fable literature drawing primarily on the Pañcatantra as its source, while reorganizing and adapting the material into a distinct new form. Tradition credits its authorship to **Nārāyaṇa**. Its dating is debated, but a widely supported view places the composition of its surviving form in Bengal around the 10th–12th centuries CE. As described in its own introduction, the work, like the Pañcatantra before it, presents itself as teaching material composed to instruct a king's sons in practical worldly wisdom.

## The Text: A Famous Verse on Universal Kinship

Let's look at the following verse, placed near the beginning of Book 1 ("Mitra-lābha," "The Gaining of Friends") of the Hitopadeśa, which is frequently quoted on its own.

```
अयं निजः परो वेति गणना लघुचेतसाम् ।
उदारचरितानां तु वसुधैव कुटुम्बकम् ॥

ayaṃ nijaḥ paro veti gaṇanā laghucetasām |
udāracaritānāṃ tu vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam ||
```

**Word-by-word gloss**

| Devanāgarī | IAST | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| अयम् | ayam | demonstrative pronoun "this," masc. nom. sg. | this one is |
| निजः | nijaḥ | nija "one's own (person)," nom. sg. | kin |
| परः | paraḥ | para "other," nom. sg. | a stranger |
| वा इति | vā iti | vā "or" + iti (quotative particle) | — or so [thinking] |
| गणना | gaṇanā | gaṇanā "reckoning/distinction," nom. sg. | such reckoning |
| लघुचेतसाम् | laghu-cetasām | compound of laghu "small/narrow" + cetas "mind," genitive pl. | belongs to narrow-minded people |
| उदारचरितानाम् | udāra-caritānām | compound of udāra "generous/noble" + carita "conduct," genitive pl. | for those of noble conduct |
| तु | tu | contrastive particle | but |
| वसुधा | vasudhā | vasudhā "earth," nom. sg. | the earth (world) |
| एव | eva | emphatic particle | truly/indeed |
| कुटुम्बकम् | kuṭumbakam | kuṭumbaka "family," nom. sg. | is one family |

**Translation:** "Thinking 'this one is kin, that one a stranger' is the reckoning of narrow minds. For those of noble conduct, the whole earth is truly one family."

## Relationship and Differences with the Pañcatantra

One major structural difference is that the Hitopadeśa does not follow the Pañcatantra's five-part division, instead reorganizing its material into four books: **Mitra-lābha** ("the gaining of friends"), **Suhṛd-bheda** ("the loss of friends"), **Sandhi** ("peace"), and **Vigraha** ("war"). While the Hitopadeśa inherits many fables directly from the Pañcatantra, it also weaves in verses drawn from legal codes (such as the *Manusmṛti*) and other collections of maxims (*subhāṣita*) throughout, giving it a strongly **anthology-like character that actively incorporates quotations from multiple earlier sources**. In fact, this very line — "the earth is one family," *vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam* — is also known to appear in a later Upaniṣadic text called the *Mahā Upaniṣad*, suggesting that such proverbial verses circulated and were shared across multiple texts of the period.

The Hitopadeśa was, in fact, one of the texts often translated and introduced in Europe even before the Pañcatantra during the early days of Sanskrit studies from the 19th century onward, and it has long held a place as a standard text widely used in early Sanskrit instruction.

## The Modern Resonance of "Vasudhaiva Kuṭumbakam"

The phrase "वसुधैव कुटुम्बकम् vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam" ("the earth is truly one family") is still frequently quoted in contemporary India as a slogan expressing ideals of international cooperation and multicultural coexistence. The idea of setting aside a narrow sense of kinship to see the entire world as a single community is a rare example of a phrase from an old work of practical fable literature continuing to live on within modern public discourse.
