## What Are the Amarna Letters?

The **Amarna letters** are a corpus of 14th-century BCE (Amarna period) diplomatic correspondence discovered at the Egyptian site of **Tell el-Amarna** (ancient Akhetaten, the new capital founded by Pharaoh Akhenaten). They were found by chance in 1887 by a local woman digging for sebakh (a nitrogen-rich soil used as fertilizer); roughly 380 tablets are known today, conventionally cited with an "EA" (El-Amarna) number, and they are now dispersed among Berlin, the British Museum, and Cairo, among other collections.

The correspondents include the Egyptian pharaoh himself; the kings of the era's other "great powers" — Babylonia, Assyria, Mitanni, and Ḫatti; and numerous rulers and chiefs of city-states across the Levant (roughly modern Syria, Lebanon, Israel, and Palestine). These Levantine polities were largely vassals of Egypt, and many of the letters express loyalty to the Egyptian court, request military assistance, or accuse rival local rulers of disloyalty.

The Akkadian of the Amarna letters was written by scribes whose native language was Canaanite — the Northwest Semitic language family ancestral to Hebrew and Phoenician — who had learned Akkadian as the international lingua franca of the day. As a result, it constitutes what is called **peripheral Akkadian**: a form of the language that departs substantially from mainstream Babylonian or Assyrian usage under the influence of the scribes' native tongue. This interference shows up throughout the grammar and vocabulary, and in many cases scribes even inserted a **Canaanite gloss** — a Canaanite word clarifying a difficult Akkadian term — directly into the text. These glosses are extraordinarily valuable evidence for reconstructing Canaanite, a language group with very little other surviving textual evidence from this period.

## The Standard Opening Formula

Most Amarna letters open with a formulaic greeting along these lines.

<p class="cuneiform">𒀀𒈾 𒈗 𒁁𒇷𒅀 𒆠𒉈𒈠</p>

```
a-na    LUGAL         be-li-ia         qi2-bi2-ma
to      king-GEN       lord-GEN+my     say-IMP+and
"To the king, my lord, say:"
```

This formula is typically followed by *umma* PN-*ma* ("thus says PN," where PN is the sender's name), which then introduces the body of the letter.

## Word-by-Word Analysis

| Transliteration | Cuneiform | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| a-na | 𒀀𒈾 | Preposition (governs the genitive case) | to, for |
| LUGAL | 𒈗 | A Sumerogram (logogram); here read as the Akkadian genitive `šarri`, "of the king" | king (gen.) |
| be-li-ia | 𒁁𒇷𒅀 | `bēlu` "lord" genitive `bēlī` + 1sg genitive possessive suffix `-ya`, "my" | my lord (gen.) |
| qi₂-bi₂-ma | 𒆠𒉈𒈠 | G-stem 2ms imperative `qibi` of `qabû`, "to say" + connective particle `-ma` | say! |

## Grammatical Notes

- **The preposition `ana` and the genitive**: Unlike the cognate prepositions in many other Semitic languages, Akkadian's prepositions `ana` ("to") and `ina` ("in") govern the **genitive** case. Both `LUGAL` (`šarri`, "of the king") and `bēlīja` ("of my lord") here are genitive.
- **The Sumerogram `LUGAL`**: Cuneiform writing widely used Sumerian-derived logograms (Sumerograms) to write nouns, while pronouncing them in Akkadian. `LUGAL` is the Sumerogram for "king," read in this context as the Akkadian genitive form `šarri`.
- **The possessive suffix `-ya`**: `bēlī-ya`, "my lord," combines the genitive of `bēlu`, "lord," with the first-person singular possessive suffix `-ya` (which appears as `-ya` after the vowel `-ī`). It stands in apposition to `šarri`, restating "the king" as "my lord."
- **Imperative + `-ma`**: `qibīma`, "say," is the G-stem imperative of `qabû`, "to say," with the connective particle `-ma` attached, linking it forward to the following clause "thus says PN." This is a fixed formulaic sequence throughout the Amarna corpus.
