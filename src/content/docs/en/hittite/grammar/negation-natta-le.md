## Two Main Negators

Hittite has two main negators, chosen according to the **mood** of the clause being negated.

- **natta**: negates declarative statements (statements of fact), corresponding to "not."
- **lē**: negates commands and requests, expressing prohibition, "don't."

These two negators are not interchangeable; the choice between them is determined mechanically by the mood of the sentence.

## Negating Declaratives with natta

```
natta ú-e-mi-ya-ḫu-un
not find-1SG.PRET
"I did not find (it)"
```

natta is also very often written with the Akkadogram **UL** — the Akkadian negator borrowed wholesale as a logographic spelling, another example of the borrowed logograms discussed in [Sumerograms and Akkadograms](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/sumerograms-and-akkadograms/).

```
UL-ma ú-iz-zi
not-but come-3SG.PRES
"but he does not come"
```

## Prohibition with lē

```
lē i-ya-ši
don't do-2SG.PRES
"don't (you) do (it)"
```

lē is used with the imperative (or a present-tense form used imperatively) to express the speaker's request that the addressee not perform some action. While natta negates a fact, lē negates a wish or demand — a functional split that keeps the two clearly distinct.

## A Pattern Shared Across Indo-European

Distinguishing a declarative negator from a prohibitive (imperative) negator is a pattern found broadly across the older Indo-European languages, not just Hittite. Latin non (declarative) versus nē (prohibitive), Classical Greek ou (declarative) versus mē (prohibitive), and Sanskrit na (declarative) versus mā (prohibitive) all correspond functionally to Hittite natta versus lē. This correspondence is treated as important evidence in comparative linguistics that the declarative/prohibitive negator distinction already existed at the Proto-Indo-European stage.
