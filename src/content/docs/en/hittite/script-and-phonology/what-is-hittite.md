## Hittite at a Glance

Hittite is the **earliest attested Indo-European language** to survive in written form. It was recorded in cuneiform as the official language of the Hittite Kingdom in central Anatolia (modern central Turkey) during the second millennium BCE.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Region spoken | Central Anatolia (modern central Turkey); capital at Ḫattuša (modern Boğazköy/Boğazkale) |
| Attested period | c. 17th century BCE – c. 1180 BCE (the fall of the Hittite Kingdom) |
| Family | Anatolian branch of Indo-European (the earliest branch to split off) |
| Script | Cuneiform borrowed from Mesopotamia (a syllabary plus Sumerograms and Akkadograms) |
| Basic word order | Basically SOV (subject–object–verb), though clause-initial connective particles carry chains of enclitics |
| Typology | Two genders (common/animate vs. neuter), two verbal conjugations (mi-conjugation and hi-conjugation), and an ergative-like marking for neuter subjects of transitive verbs |
| Discovery & decipherment | Mass tablet finds at Ḫattuša from 1906 onward; in 1915 the Czech scholar Bedřich Hrozný demonstrated that Hittite was an Indo-European language |

## The Anatolian Branch of Indo-European

Hittite belongs to the **Anatolian branch** of Indo-European, alongside Luwian, Palaic, Lycian, Lydian, and Carian. Anatolian is generally thought to have split off from Proto-Indo-European earlier than any other branch — a view sometimes called the "Indo-Hittite hypothesis" (proposed by Edgar Sturtevant). Hittite preserves a feature that proved decisive for reconstructing Proto-Indo-European phonology: the direct written attestation of [laryngeal consonants (ḫ)](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/laryngeals-in-hittite/), confirming the "laryngeal theory" that Ferdinand de Saussure had predicted purely on theoretical grounds in the late 19th century — a landmark moment in the history of linguistics.

## Chronological Stages

Hittite texts are conventionally divided into three stages based on grammar and orthography.

| Stage | Approximate period | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Old Hittite | c. 17th–15th century BCE | The earliest texts, such as the *Anitta Text*; often transmitted through later copies |
| Middle Hittite | c. 15th–14th century BCE | A transitional stage with grammar and spelling in flux |
| New (Neo-) Hittite | c. 14th–13th century BCE | Texts from the height of the empire; most surviving manuscripts reflect this stage |

## The Script and Its Origin

Hittite had no native script of its own; it was written using [cuneiform borrowed from Mesopotamia](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/cuneiform-hittite-writing-system/). In addition to the syllabic system inherited from Sumerian and Akkadian, Hittite scribes made heavy use of borrowed logograms called [Sumerograms and Akkadograms](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/sumerograms-and-akkadograms/). This means Hittite largely inherited the Akkadian scribal conventions wholesale while adapting them to write a very differently structured Indo-European language.

## Surviving Texts

The tens of thousands of clay tablet fragments recovered from the royal archives at Ḫattuša include royal annals, [treaties with neighboring states](/en/hittite/readings/kbo-treaty-formula/), the [Hittite Laws](/en/hittite/readings/hittite-laws-excerpt/), ritual texts, myths (such as the [Illuyanka myth](/en/hittite/readings/illuyanka-myth-excerpt/)), and prayers (such as the [plague prayers](/en/hittite/readings/plague-prayers-mursili/)). Because clay is such a durable medium, many tablets were accidentally "fired" hard when the palace burned down — a destructive event that, ironically, helped preserve them for millennia.
