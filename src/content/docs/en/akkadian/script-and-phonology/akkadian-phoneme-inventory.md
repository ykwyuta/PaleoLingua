## Consonant Phonemes

Akkadian's consonants are traditionally organized as below. Cuneiform syllabic signs do not represent a single phoneme in isolation, but the table shows a representative CV (consonant + vowel) sign containing each phoneme.

| Place/manner | Phoneme | Cuneiform | Transliteration | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bilabial stop | /p/ | 𒉺 | pa | Voiceless |
| Bilabial stop | /b/ | 𒁀 | ba | Voiced |
| Dental/alveolar stop | /t/ | 𒋫 | ta | Voiceless |
| Dental/alveolar stop | /d/ | 𒋫 | da | Voiced. Can be written with the same glyph as `t` |
| Dental/alveolar stop (emphatic) | /ṭ/ | 𒋫 | ṭa | An emphatic consonant, thought to be pharyngealized or ejective. Can be written with the same glyph as `t`/`d` |
| Velar stop | /k/ | 𒆉 | ka | Voiceless |
| Velar stop | /g/ | 𒂵 | ga | Voiced |
| Uvular stop (emphatic) | /q/ | 𒋡 | qa | An emphatic consonant articulated further back than `k` |
| Glottal stop | /ʾ/ | (no dedicated sign) | ʾ | Has no independent sign in the syllabary; often left unwritten or absorbed into a vowel sign |
| Pharyngeal/velar fricative | /ḫ/ | 𒄩 | ḫa | A voiceless fricative, roughly like German ch |
| Dental/alveolar fricative | /s/ | 𒊓 | sa | Voiceless |
| Dental/alveolar fricative (emphatic) | /ṣ/ | 𒍝 | ṣa | An emphatic consonant. Can be written with the same glyph as `z` |
| Dental/alveolar fricative | /z/ | 𒍝 | za | Voiced. Can be written with the same glyph as `ṣ` |
| Postalveolar fricative | /š/ | 𒊭 | ša | Close to English "sh" |
| Nasal | /m/ | 𒈠 | ma | |
| Nasal | /n/ | 𒈾 | na | |
| Liquid | /l/ | 𒆷 | la | |
| Liquid | /r/ | 𒊏 | ra | |
| Glide | /w/ | 𒉿 | wa | Can be written with the same glyph (PI) as `y` |
| Glide | /y/ | 𒉿 | ya | Can be written with the same glyph (PI) as `w` |

As the table shows, pairs like `t`/`d`/`ṭ` (identical glyph) and `ṣ`/`z` (identical glyph) illustrate, in exactly the place you'd expect (the voiceless/voiced/emphatic three-way contrast), the "one glyph, several phonemes" phenomenon discussed in [The Cuneiform Writing System for Akkadian](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/cuneiform-akkadian-writing-system/).

## What Are the Emphatic Consonants (ṣ, ṭ, q)?

/ṣ/, /ṭ/, and /q/ form a third series alongside the corresponding voiceless consonants /s/, /t/, /k/, traditionally called the "emphatic" consonants. Their exact phonetic realization — whether uvularized/pharyngealized, as in Arabic, or glottalized (ejective), as reconstructed for some other Semitic branches — remains debated in comparative Semitic phonology. What is broadly agreed, however, is that at the phonemic level Akkadian (like other Semitic languages) organizes several of its consonant series into three-way voiceless/voiced/emphatic contrasts.

## Vowel Phonemes

Akkadian has four vowel qualities — a, i, u, e — each contrasting a short and a long form.

| Vowel | Cuneiform | Transliteration |
|---|---|---|
| /a/ | 𒀀 | a |
| /i/ | 𒄿 | i |
| /u/ | 𒕢 | u |
| /e/ | 𒂊 | e |

Long vowels are marked in transliteration with a macron (ā, ī, ū, ē) and function as phonemes distinct from their short counterparts. In the cuneiform spelling itself, a long vowel is often shown by doubling up the vowel sign — so-called "plene" spelling. A few examples with long vowels follow.

<p class="cuneiform">𒈠𒀀𒌈</p>

```
mātum
land, country (long ā in the second syllable)
```

<p class="cuneiform">𒁉𒄿𒌈</p>

```
bītum
house (long ī in the first syllable, plene spelling)
```

<p class="cuneiform">𒋗𒕢</p>

```
šū
he (independent pronoun; the doubled u marks long ū, plene spelling)
```

## Geers' Law (Dissimilation of Emphatics)

A frequently cited rule in the study of Akkadian roots is **Geers' Law** (named for F. W. Geers), a dissimilation phenomenon. It states that a single triconsonantal root does not tolerate two emphatic consonants (ṣ, ṭ, q): where comparative Semitic evidence would lead one to expect two emphatics in the same root, Akkadian regularly shows one of them replaced by its corresponding plain, non-emphatic consonant (s, t, or k). This pattern emerges from comparing forms reconstructed via comparison with other Semitic languages against the forms actually attested in Akkadian texts, and it is one of the well-established regularities in the phonology of Akkadian roots.
