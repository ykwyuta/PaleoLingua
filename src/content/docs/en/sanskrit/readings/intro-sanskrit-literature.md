## Three Thousand Years of Unbroken Literature

The history of Sanskrit literature stretches back to the hymns of the **Ṛgveda**, thought to have been composed around 1200 BCE, and continues without a real break into the 20th century — a continuity rarely matched in human history. This "Readings & Examples" section selects a set of especially important works and documents from that vast field, using excerpts from the original texts to build the foundations of reading comprehension. Below is a map of where each of the articles that follow fits within the larger history of Sanskrit literature.

## Major Genres and Periods

| Classical kāvya (belles-lettres) and drama (flourishing especially 1st–7th c. ## Oral Transmission and Writing: Sanskrit's Unusual Transmission History

A crucial fact for understanding the history of Sanskrit literature is the enormous weight placed on **oral transmission independent of writing**. The prevailing scholarly view holds that Vedic texts were transmitted from teacher to student through rigorous memorization — including exact pronunciation and pitch accent — long before Pāṇini's systematization of Sanskrit grammar (c. 4th century BCE), and that they were committed to writing only much later. Specialized recitation techniques that permitted no error in even a single syllable developed to support this, including *padapāṭha* ("word-by-word recitation") and *kramapāṭha* ("sequential recitation"), which are thought to have preserved the texts' fidelity across millennia.

The epics and fable literature, by contrast, were not bound by rules of memorization as strict as those governing the Vedas, and different recensions coexist across periods and regions. Manuscripts of the *Mahābhārata* run to tens of thousands of verses and vary considerably in content; the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute (BORI) in Pune spent decades in the 20th century producing a "critical edition" reconciling these traditions.

## Vedic Sanskrit and Classical Sanskrit

Linguistically, scholars often distinguish between **Vedic Sanskrit** — found in the Vedic corpus, preserving archaic phonology, morphology, freer word order, and pitch accent — and **Classical Sanskrit**, standardized by Pāṇini's grammar, the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, and used in most texts from the epics onward. The two are essentially different stages of the same language, but there are non-trivial differences in vocabulary, morphology, and syntax between them. This section covers both Vedic-period hymns (the Ṛgveda, the Upaniṣads) and classical works (the epics, kāvya, fable literature, inscriptions), so readers should keep in mind which linguistic stage a given text belongs to.
