## What is Njáls saga?

*Njáls saga* (also called *Brennu-Njáls saga*, "The Saga of Burnt Njáll") is a long prose work, generally dated to the late 13th century in Iceland, and is widely regarded as the finest of the *Íslendingasögur* ("Sagas of Icelanders"). Set in Iceland at the turn of the 10th and 11th centuries, it traces a chain of honor, feud, and courtroom conflict centered on the wise lawman Njáll Þorgeirsson and his friend Gunnar. Written in prose, its style differs from the verse of the [Poetic Edda](/en/readings/eddic-poetry-intro/), but it stands as an essential document of standard Old Norse prose.

## The opening lines

Saga literature has a recognizable convention for briskly introducing a character, and the opening of *Njáls saga* is a textbook example of it.

```
Mǫrðr  hét  maðr,  er  kallaðr  var  gígja.
Hann  var  sonr  Sighvats  hins rauða.
```

**Word-by-word gloss (sentence 1)**

| Mǫrðr | hét | maðr | er | kallaðr | var | gígja |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mǫrðr(NOM) | was named | a man(NOM) | who | called | was | "Fiddle" |

**Word-by-word gloss (sentence 2)**

| Hann | var | sonr | Sighvats | hins rauða |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| he(NOM) | was | son(NOM) | of-Sighvatr(GEN) | the red(GEN) |

**Translation**: "There was a man named Mörðr, who was called Fiddle. He was the son of Sighvatr the Red."

## Grammar notes

- `hét` ("was named") is the past tense of the verb *heita* ("to be called," "to be named"), the standard formula for introducing a saga character. Note that the name *Mǫrðr* stays in the nominative — unlike an English passive ("was named"), this remains a grammatically active construction in Old Norse.
- The `er` in `er kallaðr var gígja` is the invariant relative particle, "who/that." `kallaðr` is the past participle (masculine nominative) of *kalla* ("to call"), and combines with `var` ("was") to form a passive meaning.
- `gígja` ("fiddle," a stringed instrument) is used here as a nickname. Saga characters very frequently carry a nickname alongside their given name, often drawn from appearance, temperament, or a memorable episode.
- `hins rauða` ("the red") combines `hins`, the genitive of the article *hinn*, with `rauða`, the weak genitive of the adjective *rauðr* ("red") — producing the nickname-like phrase "[Sighvatr] the Red." It's a real-world instance of the free-standing article *hinn* discussed in "[The Suffixed Definite Article](/en/grammar/suffixed-definite-article/)."

## The style of saga narration

Even this short opening reveals the hallmark of saga style: spare, largely unadorned prose. Emotion and interiority are rarely stated directly; instead, the narrative advances through action, dialogue, and a careful accumulation of relationships — whose son someone is, whom they married. This restrained narrative voice, often credited with influencing later prose fiction, is one of saga literature's defining features.
