## "Common/Neuter," Not "Masculine/Feminine"

Many early Indo-European languages — Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, and more — distinguish three genders: masculine, feminine, and neuter. Hittite (and the other Anatolian languages), however, distinguishes only two: **common** and **neuter**. Common gender covers nouns traditionally considered "animate" — people, animals, and the like — while neuter covers nouns traditionally considered "inanimate" — things, liquids, abstract concepts.

## Why Call It "Common"?

The label "common" reflects the fact that this gender class includes both masculine and feminine referents without distinction. The words for "son" and "daughter," for instance, are both grammatically common-gender nouns following the same declension pattern. Grammatical gender and the biological sex of a referent are separate matters here — even with a common-gender noun, biological sex is only made explicit through context or a modifying word (corresponding to "male" or "female").

## Hypotheses About the Origin of the Two-Gender System

Two broad views compete regarding the origin of the Anatolian two-gender system:

- **The archaism hypothesis**: Proto-Indo-European originally had a two-way animate/inanimate gender system, and the Anatolian branch simply preserved this older stage. Under this view, the masculine/feminine distinction developed later in the other branches, after Anatolian had already split off.
- **The reduction hypothesis**: Proto-Indo-European already had three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), and in (pre-)Anatolian the masculine and feminine merged, simplifying the system to two genders.

The archaism hypothesis fits naturally with the "Indo-Hittite hypothesis," which treats Anatolian as the earliest branch to split from Proto-Indo-European, and serves as one of its key pieces of supporting evidence. Which view is correct remains actively debated, with no settled conclusion.

## The Syntactic Peculiarity of Neuter Nouns

Neuter nouns behave differently from common-gender nouns not only semantically but syntactically. The most striking difference is the special case marking (the ergative) that appears when a neuter noun functions as the subject of a transitive verb — a phenomenon not found with common-gender nouns. See [The Neuter Ergative Case](/en/hittite/grammar/neuter-ergative-case/) for details.

```
Common-gender example: LUGAL-uš (the king, nom.)
Neuter example: wātar (water, nom. = acc.)
```
