## Background: The Trading Colony at Kaneš

Around the 19th–18th centuries BCE, merchants from the city of Aššur in northern Mesopotamia maintained a permanent trading settlement — in Akkadian, a **kārum** (*kārum*, "quay, trading colony") — at the Anatolian city of Kaneš (the modern site of Kültepe, in central Turkey). These merchants ran a long-distance trade network, sending donkey caravans loaded with tin and textiles from Aššur into Anatolia and returning with local silver and gold.

Twentieth-century excavations of the merchant quarter at Kaneš uncovered more than 20,000 cuneiform tablets. The great majority of these are **private archives** belonging to individual merchant families — letters, contracts, loan documents, and court records — rather than royal or temple records. This makes the corpus unique: it preserves the everyday business dealings and family affairs of ordinary merchants in vivid, unfiltered detail. The dialect of Akkadian used in this period and region is known as **Old Assyrian**, distinguished from later Assyrian and Babylonian by its own phonological and lexical features.

## The Letter's Opening Formula

Akkadian letters in general — not only Old Assyrian ones — typically open with the sender instructing a messenger (or scribe) to relay a message: "say to (the recipient)..." A representative skeleton of this formula looks like:

> umma PN₁-ma / ana PN₂ qibi-ma
> "(Sender) PN₁ says: Speak to PN₂!"

Below is the core command — "speak to..." — that recurs across letters, shown with verified cuneiform.

<p class="cuneiform">𒀀𒈾 𒁁𒉌𒅀 𒆠𒉈𒈠</p>

```
a-na    be-lí-ia    qí-bí-ma
to      my lord      speak-(emphatic -ma)
```

"Speak to my lord" — this phrase is placed right before the letter's body, addressing the person who will actually read (or read aloud) the message. `qí-bí-ma` 𒆠𒉈𒈠 is the imperative *qibi* of the verb *qabûm* ("to say"), with the enclitic particle **-ma** attached to highlight the utterance. This same *-ma* recurs constantly in Old Assyrian letter formulas, often in the pattern "umma PN-ma" ("PN says").

## Trade Vocabulary

Merchant letters repeat a set of terms specific to commerce. Two representative examples:

| Transliteration | Cuneiform | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ka-ru-um | 𒅗𒊒𒌝 | kārum (stem kār- + nominative -um) | trading colony, "quay" |
| kás-pu-um | 𒁉𒁍𒌝 | kaspum (stem kasp- + nominative -um) | silver |

`kārum` 𒅗𒊒𒌝 literally meant a river "quay," but came to designate the overseas trading post the Assyrian merchants established in Anatolia, and by extension the merchant-community organization that administered it. `kaspum` 𒁉𒁍𒌝 "silver" functioned as a near-monetary medium of exchange in this period, and merchant letters constantly discuss its weight, purity, and the terms of a transaction.

## Why These Letters Matter

The Kaneš merchant archives are valuable for two distinct reasons.

First, they are a first-rate **source for social and economic history**. Where royal inscriptions and law codes articulate official state ideology, merchant letters report the actual terms of business deals, disputes over the division of family property, and news about wives and children left behind at home. They are an unparalleled window onto early commercial practices, including credit arrangements and joint-investment partnerships (*nāruqqum*, "investment fund").

Second, they are an invaluable **record of the Old Assyrian dialect**. Old Assyrian shows phonological and grammatical features distinct from contemporary Old Babylonian, spoken in southern Mesopotamia, and it is the direct ancestor of later Assyrian. Because private letters tend to preserve language closer to everyday speech than public inscriptions do, they are an especially rich resource for dialectology.
