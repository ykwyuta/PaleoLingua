## The World's Longest Epic

The **Mahābhārata** ("The Great [Tale] of the Bhārata Dynasty") is said to exceed 100,000 verses (ślokas), making it one of the longest epics to survive from the classical world. Its central plot concerns a succession dispute between two branches of the Kuru dynasty, the Pāṇḍava and Kaurava brothers, culminating in the great war at Kurukṣetra — but woven into that frame are countless subplots, myths, legal and ethical treatises, and works of practical wisdom, the Bhagavad Gītā among them. The text famously makes a self-referential claim (found in Book 1) that "whatever is found here may be found elsewhere, but whatever is not found here exists nowhere" — a statement of its own comprehensiveness. Its composition spans a long period: the oral core is thought to reach back to around 400 BCE, while the text is generally believed to have reached something close to its surviving form only by around the 4th century CE.

## The Ancient Title "Jaya" and Layers of Transmission

In older tradition, the Mahābhārata is frequently referred to by the name **"Jaya"** ("Victory"). According to traditional accounts, the story was first told by the sage **Vyāsa**, then expanded and transmitted through generations of his disciples. Because many manuscripts and regional recensions survive side by side, the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute (BORI) in Pune spent decades in the 20th century comparing northern and southern manuscript traditions to reconstruct their common oldest layer, producing the **Critical Edition** (published 1919–1966). It should be noted, however, that the Critical Edition represents one scholarly reconstruction among possible approaches, not a definitively established "single correct text."

## The Text: The Traditional Opening Invocation

Let's look at the well-known invocatory (*maṅgala*) verse that many vulgate editions place before the text proper begins.

```
नारायणं नमस्कृत्य नरं चैव नरोत्तमम् ।
देवीं सरस्वतीं चैव ततो जयमुदीरयेत् ॥

nārāyaṇaṃ namaskṛtya naraṃ caiva narottamam |
devīṃ sarasvatīṃ caiva tato jayam udīrayet ||
```

**Word-by-word gloss**

| Devanāgarī | IAST | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| नारायणम् | nārāyaṇam | Nārāyaṇa (an epithet of Viṣṇu), acc. sg. | to Nārāyaṇa (the god) |
| नमस्कृत्य | namaskṛtya | namas "homage" + √kṛ "to do/make," absolutive (gerund) | having paid homage |
| नरम् | naram | nara "man," acc. sg. (here the proper name of the sage Nara) | to Nara (the sage) |
| च एव | ca eva | conjunction + emphatic particle | and also |
| नरोत्तमम् | nara-uttamam | compound of nara "man" + uttama "highest," acc. sg. | to the foremost among men |
| देवीम् | devīm | devī "goddess," acc. sg. | to the goddess |
| सरस्वतीम् | sarasvatīm | Sarasvatī (goddess of learning and eloquence), acc. sg. | to (goddess) Sarasvatī |
| ततः | tataḥ | adverb | then |
| जयम् | jayam | jaya "victory," acc. sg. (also this work's ancient title) | "Jaya" (victory) |
| उदीरयेत् | udīrayet | ud-√īr "to utter/set forth," 3sg. optative | one should utter |

**Translation:** "Having paid homage to Nārāyaṇa (Viṣṇu), and to Nara, foremost among men, and also to the goddess Sarasvatī, one should then utter 'Jaya.'"

## Grammar Note: The Absolutive, and the Title "Jaya"

This verse uses one of the most common Sanskrit constructions, the **absolutive (gerund)**, formed with the suffix -tvā or -ya. **नमस्कृत्य namaskṛtya** ("having paid homage") is a textbook example: it denotes an action completed prior to that of the main verb (here, *udīrayet*, "one should utter"), concisely expressing a sequential relationship — "having done X, [then do Y]."

As this verse shows, the Mahābhārata is frequently referred to by the ancient title **"Jaya."** Scholars often note the hypothesis that an original core narrative (called "Jaya") was later expanded into the Mahābhārata as we now have it, but the precise process and stratigraphy of that expansion remain unsettled, with multiple competing accounts still in circulation.

## What the Text's Sheer Scale Signifies

The current scholarly consensus treats the epic's 100,000-plus verses not as the work of a single author writing at one sitting, but as the product of generations of reciters and redactors who retold and expanded the story over time. Even this opening invocatory verse itself varies in position — and in whether it appears at all — across different manuscripts, a fact that itself illustrates the fluid transmission history of the Mahābhārata as a text.
