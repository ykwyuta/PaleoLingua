## Background: "autobiographies" and the offering formula on tomb walls

Egyptian tomb walls and stelae frequently combine two elements. The first is an "autobiography" — a first-person inscription in which the deceased lists his own titles and praiseworthy deeds, aiming to preserve his reputation and virtue for posterity. The second is the standardized prayer known as *ḥtp-dj-nsw* ("an offering which the king gives"), which asks that offerings — bread, beer, cattle, fowl, and the like — be provided in perpetuity for the deceased's ka (kꜣ), his life-force. This formula often addresses a god (frequently Osiris or Anubis) who actually grants the offerings on the king's behalf.

The *ḥtp-dj-nsw* formula is one of the most frequently attested phrases in the entire corpus of Egyptian inscriptions; people of every social rank who could afford an inscription tended to have this prayer carved on their tomb.

## Example: the skeleton of the offering formula

The phrase below is a short illustrative example showing the structure of the *ḥtp-dj-nsw* formula, built from vocabulary that recurs throughout the genre — not a verbatim quotation from any one specific inscription.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓎛𓏏𓊪 𓂧𓇋 𓈖𓋴𓅱 … 𓈖 𓎡𓄿</p>

```
ḥtp     dj       nsw     ...   n      kꜣ
offering give     king    ...   for    ka
```

*ḥtp dj nsw* means literally "an offering which the king gives," and the formula continues with a phrase naming the deceased (or their ka) as the recipient. Here we've spelled *nsw* ("king") using the purely phonetic worked-example spelling (n-s-w); real inscriptions, however, typically follow a scribal convention called "honorific transposition," placing the sign for the royal sedge-plant at the very front of the group out of respect, even though it is read last in pronunciation (this convention is covered in more detail in [Script and Phonology: Writing Direction and Orientation](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/writing-direction-orientation/)).

## Why the formula mattered so much

This formula was used so pervasively because ensuring an unbroken supply of offerings after death was a central concern for the Egyptians. Since it was impossible to actually deliver offerings daily forever, the prayer itself was believed to carry a kind of magical efficacy: simply reciting or reading it aloud was thought to supply the offerings in place of the real thing.
