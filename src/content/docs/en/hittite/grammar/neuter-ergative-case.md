## Why Neuter Nouns Need a Special Case

As we saw in [An Overview of Hittite Noun Declension](/en/hittite/grammar/noun-declension-overview/), Hittite neuter nouns show identical nominative and accusative forms. This is a feature shared broadly across Indo-European, but a problem arises when a neuter noun needs to serve as the **subject of a transitive verb**: with the nominative and accusative forms identical, there is no way to tell from the shape of the word alone whether it is the doer ("subject") or the thing done to ("object").

To resolve this ambiguity, Hittite (and Anatolian more broadly) developed a special case ending that appears only when a neuter noun functions as the subject of a transitive verb. This is the **ergative** case.

## The Ergative Ending -anza

The neuter ergative is most often marked with the ending **-anza** (singular).

```
ut-tar
word (neuter, nom.=acc.)
"the word" (subject or object)

ut-tar-anza
word-ERG
"the word" (as subject of a transitive verb)
```

The neuter noun uttar "word," for example, can stay in its ordinary nominative=accusative form uttar when it is simply the subject of an intransitive verb ("the word exists"). But when it functions as the subject of a transitive verb — "the word establishes/determines (something)" — the ergative form uttar-anza can be used instead.

## Where the Ergative Fits: Split Ergativity

While common-gender nouns distinguish subject and object through the nominative/accusative contrast, neuter nouns build on a shared nominative=accusative base and require an additional case marking (the ergative) only when they serve as transitive subjects. This pattern — where case-marking strategy switches depending on the noun's class (gender) — is typologically classified as a form of **split ergativity**. Note that Hittite as a whole is not an ergative language; ergative-like behavior is observed only within the limited domain of neuter nouns.

## Limited Attestation and Ongoing Debate

Actual attestations of the ergative form are not especially abundant in the surviving corpus, and reliable examples of the plural ergative ending in particular are scarce, leaving scholars divided on its exact shape and productivity. Whether every neuter noun necessarily takes the ergative form when serving as a transitive subject, or whether the phenomenon is limited to particular words or constructions, also remains under careful investigation.
