## The Sumerian "Gilgamesh" is not a single epic

Unlike the later Akkadian *Epic of Gilgamesh* (the Standard Babylonian version), compiled onto twelve tablets, the Gilgamesh material transmitted in Sumerian was originally composed as five independent poems. These survive today mainly through manuscripts from the Ur III and Old Babylonian periods (roughly the 21st-18th centuries BCE), especially copies excavated at the scribal center of Nippur.

| Poem (conventional English title) | Summary |
|---|---|
| Gilgamesh and Huwawa (the Land of the Living) | Gilgamesh's expedition to defeat Huwawa, guardian of the cedar forest |
| Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven | Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay the "Bull of Heaven," unleashed by the goddess Inana |
| Gilgamesh and Aga | King Aga of Kish besieges Uruk, and Gilgamesh's response |
| Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld | Enkidu descends to the netherworld and reports back to Gilgamesh |
| The Death of Gilgamesh | King Gilgamesh's death and its aftermath |

These five poems are generally thought to be the direct or indirect sources for the major episodes of the later Akkadian epic (the defeat of Huwawa, the Bull of Heaven, Enkidu's death and its aftermath, and so on), but the details of the plot and characterization differ considerably from version to version — this is not a simple "original and translation" relationship.

## A formulaic title: "King of Uruk"

Throughout these poems, and in the later Sumerian King List as well, Gilgamesh is repeatedly called by the formulaic title "king of Uruk."

```
𒈗 𒀕𒆠𒂵
lugal   unugki-ga
lugal   unug{ki}-ak
king    Uruk-GEN
"king of Uruk"
```

This title is built with exactly the "noun + possessor + `-ak`" structure covered in [The Genitive -ak](/en/sumerian/grammar/genitive-case-ak/): following the final `g` of `unug` (Uruk), `-ak` surfaces as `-ga` (a doubled consonant plus `-a`).
