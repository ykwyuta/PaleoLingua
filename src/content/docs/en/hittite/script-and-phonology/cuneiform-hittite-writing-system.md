## Borrowed from Mesopotamia

The Hittites never invented a script of their own; instead they borrowed cuneiform, the writing system developed in Mesopotamia, to record their language. This borrowing is thought to have come through Assyrian merchants active in central Anatolia during the early second millennium BCE (the Old Assyrian cuneiform of the *kārum* period) and/or through a Hurrian scribal tradition from Syria and northern Mesopotamia. As a result, the shapes and conventions of Hittite cuneiform share much in common with contemporary Old Babylonian and Hurrian cuneiform practice.

Cuneiform was written on damp clay tablets by pressing a reed or wooden stylus into the surface at an angle, producing the characteristic wedge-shaped ("cuneus" is Latin for "wedge") impressions that give the script its name. Finished tablets were either left to dry in the sun or, for important documents, fired in a kiln — a durable medium for long-term record-keeping.

## Use as a Syllabary

The core of Hittite cuneiform consists of **syllabic signs** with the values V (a single vowel), CV (consonant + vowel), VC (vowel + consonant), and CVC (consonant + vowel + consonant).

| Type | Example (transliteration) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| V | a, i, u | Vowel alone |
| CV | pa, ta, ku | Consonant + vowel |
| VC | ap, it, un | Vowel + consonant |
| CVC | kur, pár, tar | Consonant + vowel + consonant (used less frequently than the other three types) |

Hittite's syllabary is drawn almost entirely from the Sumerian/Akkadian sign inventory, so words are spelled by stringing together signs that pair a given consonant with different vowels (pa, pi, pu, etc.). For the detailed transliteration conventions, see [Transliteration Conventions](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/transliteration-conventions/).

## Combined with Logograms (Sumerograms and Akkadograms)

A striking feature of Hittite texts is the heavy use, alongside syllabic signs, of **Sumerograms** — Sumerian words reused wholesale as logograms — and **Akkadograms** — Akkadian word forms reused the same way. The word for "king," for instance, could be spelled out in native Hittite syllables, but was very often written instead with the single Sumerian logogram LUGAL.

```
LUGAL-uš
king (Sumerogram) + Hittite nominative ending -uš
"the king (nom.)"
```

This kind of mixed spelling — a Sumerogram stem with a Hittite case ending tacked on in syllabic signs — was extremely common. For more detail, see [Sumerograms and Akkadograms](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/sumerograms-and-akkadograms/).

## Direction of Writing and Scribal Training

Hittite cuneiform is written left to right, with lines running from top to bottom. Scribes (denoted by the Sumerogram DUB.SAR, "one who writes tablets") trained at dedicated scribal schools, learning Sumerian and Akkadian vocabulary lists as part of mastering a complex, multilingual writing system. The archives at Ḫattuša have yielded many copies of such vocabulary lists and grammatical exercises.
