## Case Marking in Akkadian

Akkadian nouns mark their syntactic role (subject, dependent/possessed, or object) through a system of case vowels, conceptually similar to case systems in Indo-European languages. In the singular, three cases are distinguished.

| Case | Final vowel | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | -u | Subject of the clause, subject of a nominal sentence |
| Genitive | -i | Object of a preposition, the possessor following a [construct state](/en/akkadian/grammar/construct-state/) head noun |
| Accusative | -a | Direct object of a transitive verb |

In Old Babylonian (and already in earlier Old Akkadian), these singular case vowels are followed by a further final **-m**, a phenomenon known as **mimation**. In practice, the forms that actually surface are -um (nominative), -im (genitive), and -am (accusative).

## The Full Paradigm: šarrum "King"

The masculine noun `šarrum` "king" (from the root ŠRR "to rule") illustrates all three singular cases.

| Case | Transliteration | Cuneiform | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | šarrum | 𒊭𒅈𒊒𒌝 | šarr (root) + -u (nom.) + -m (mimation) | "the king (subject)" |
| Genitive | šarrim | 𒊭𒅈𒊑𒅎 | šarr + -i (gen.) + -m (mimation) | "of the king" |
| Accusative | šarram | 𒊭𒅈𒊏𒄠 | šarr + -a (acc.) + -m (mimation) | "the king (object)" |

As the table shows, the only element that changes across the three forms is the case vowel (u/i/a) immediately after the root `šarr-`; the mimation `-m` is added uniformly regardless of case. Mimation does not itself distinguish case — it is simply a final marker attached to uninflected singular nouns (and adjectives).

## The Loss of Mimation

Mimation is still written regularly in the Old Babylonian period, but it gradually disappears over time.

- From Middle Babylonian/Middle Assyrian onward, mimation is increasingly omitted in prose, and it has essentially vanished by the Neo-Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian periods.
- As mimation is lost, the case vowels themselves also tend to shorten and weaken, so that in later dialects the case system itself gradually becomes less distinct — most noticeably in prose closer to everyday speech.
- More formal registers, such as poetry and prayers, sometimes preserve mimation as an archaism for longer.

This shift is closely connected to the fact that the [construct state](/en/akkadian/grammar/construct-state/) already drops both the case ending and mimation. Historically, the general loss of mimation across the noun system is sometimes explained as a generalization of the same "drop the final elements" pattern that always applied in the construct state.

## Overview of Plural and Dual Patterns

The plural and dual follow different patterns from the three-case singular system.

| Number | Case | Typical ending | Example (from šarrum "king") | Cuneiform | 
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plural (masc.) | Nominative | -ū | šarrū "the kings (subject)" | 𒊭𒅈𒊒𒌑 |
| Plural (masc.) | Genitive/Accusative | -ī | šarrī "of the kings / the kings (object)" | 𒊭𒅈𒊑 |
| Plural (fem.) | Nominative | -ātum | from šarratum "queen," plural šarrātum "the queens (subject)" | 𒊭𒅈𒊏𒌈 |

Two points stand out in the plural: (1) the masculine plural collapses the three-way singular distinction into just two forms — nominative -ū versus a merged genitive/accusative -ī — and (2) the plural never takes mimation. The feminine plural, meanwhile, typically combines the feminine plural marker -āt- with a following case vowel.

A dual form also exists (-ān nominative / -īn genitive-accusative for pairs), but in the historical periods of Akkadian its use is largely restricted to naturally paired body parts (e.g., "two hands," "two eyes") and certain numeral expressions — it is far less productive than the singular or plural.
