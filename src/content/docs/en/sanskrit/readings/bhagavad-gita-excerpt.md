## A Philosophical Poem within the Mahābhārata

The **Bhagavad Gītā** ("Song of the Blessed One") is not an independent work but a 700-verse teaching poem transmitted as part of Book 6 (Bhīṣma Parvan) of the great epic **Mahābhārata**. The narrative frame is set on the eve of the Kurukṣetra War, a catastrophic battle between kin of the Kuru dynasty. The hero **Arjuna**, seeing his own teachers and relatives arrayed on the opposing side, loses the will to fight and confides his anguish to **Kṛṣṇa** — an incarnation of the god Viṣṇu, who has taken the role of his charioteer. In response, Kṛṣṇa lays out teachings on duty (dharma), the philosophy of action (karma yoga), the path of knowledge (jñāna yoga), and the path of devotion (bhakti yoga), among other topics. Its dating remains debated, but a broadly supported view places the text's arrival at its current form sometime between roughly the turn of the era and the 2nd century CE.

## The Text (Chapter 2, Verse 47)

Chapter 2 serves as the introduction to Kṛṣṇa's teaching, moving in its second half from the doctrinal discussion known as "Sāṅkhya Yoga" toward the practical teaching that one should let go of attachment to the results of action. The following verse is the single most widely quoted line in the entire poem.

```
कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन ।
मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि ॥

karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana |
mā karmaphalahetur bhūr mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi ||
```

**Word-by-word gloss**

| Devanāgarī | IAST | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| कर्मणि | karmaṇi | karman "action," locative sg. | in action |
| एव | eva | emphatic particle | precisely, only |
| अधिकारः | adhikāraḥ | adhikāra "right/entitlement," nom. sg. | there is a right |
| ते | te | 2nd-person pronoun "you," dative (possessive use), sg. | for you / your |
| मा | mā | prohibitive particle | do not (negative command) |
| फलेषु | phaleṣu | phala "fruit/result," locative pl. | in the results |
| कदाचन | kadācana | adverb | at any time, ever |
| कर्मफलहेतुः | karma-phala-hetuḥ | compound of karman "action" + phala "fruit" + hetu "cause/motive," nom. sg. | one whose motive is the fruit of action |
| भूः | bhūḥ | √bhū "to become," 2sg. aorist (with prohibitive mā) | become |
| सङ्गः | saṅgaḥ | saṅga "attachment," nom. sg. | attachment |
| अस्तु | astu | √as "to be," 3sg. imperative | let there be |
| अकर्मणि | akarmaṇi | a- (negative prefix) + karman "action," locative sg. | in inaction, in not acting |

**Translation:** "Your right is to action alone, never to its results. Do not become one whose motive is the fruit of action, but let there also be no attachment to inaction."

## Context: The Heart of Karma Yoga

This verse is well known as a concise crystallization of **karma yoga** ("the discipline of action"), the doctrine that runs through the entire Gītā. Kṛṣṇa does not tell Arjuna to abandon fighting altogether; rather, he teaches that Arjuna should act without clinging to the results of action — victory or defeat, honor, or gain. Combined with the doctrine set out earlier in Chapter 2 — that the Ātman (self) is imperishable and unaffected by the death of the body — this teaching provides the metaphysical grounding that makes "action free from attachment to results" possible.

Grammatically, the verse twice uses the construction **मा mā** (prohibitive particle) together with an aorist form used in an imperative sense ("mā + aorist"), a standard Classical Sanskrit means of expressing prohibition, lending the injunction a forceful tone.

## The Gītā's Place in Intellectual History

The Bhagavad Gītā is often described as a bridge between the ritual-centered religion of the Vedic period and the later forms of Hinduism that emphasize inner ethics and personal devotion (bhakti). In the modern era, it is particularly well known for having been repeatedly cited by Mahatma Gandhi as one of the textual foundations of his philosophy of nonviolence, and it remains among the most widely translated and studied Sanskrit texts outside India.
