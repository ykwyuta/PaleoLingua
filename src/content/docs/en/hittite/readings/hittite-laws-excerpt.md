## What Are the Hittite Laws?

The **Hittite Laws** are a legal document laying out concrete penalties for a wide range of matters — homicide, bodily injury, livestock theft, marriage, sorcery, and more. The surviving manuscripts comprise roughly 200 clauses, and comparative study of these clauses has shown that they preserve both original early provisions and later revised ones side by side.

## The Takku-Clause Pattern

Nearly every clause of the Hittite Laws opens with the conditional particle **takku** "if," followed by a construction along the lines of "if such-and-such happens, then such-and-such penalty/compensation is imposed." This pattern is widely shared with other Mesopotamian law codes (such as the Code of Hammurabi) and is common across ancient Near Eastern legal texts generally.

Below is a simplified example sentence, for learning purposes, illustrating the features of this formulaic construction (note that this is not a verbatim quotation of a specific clause, but a demonstration of a construction pattern that recurs frequently throughout the law code).

```
tak-ku LÚ-an ku-en-zi
if man-ACC kill-3SG.PRES
"if (someone) kills a man"

nu-uš-ši ANA DAM.ŠU EGIR-pa pa-a-i
and-to.him to his.wife instead give-3SG.PRES
"he shall give (a person, as compensation) to his wife's household instead"
```

## A Distinctive View of Punishment: An Emphasis on Compensation

One reason the Hittite Laws draw significant scholarly attention is that, compared with other contemporary ancient Near Eastern law codes, they are said to contain relatively **more clauses imposing financial or material compensation rather than corporal or capital punishment**. Compared to the Code of Hammurabi, known for its principle of retaliation in kind ("an eye for an eye"), the Hittite Laws prescribe compensation in persons, livestock, or silver for many offenses. That said, not every clause resolves purely through compensation — serious crimes, such as treason against the crown or certain sexual offenses, are still met with harsh penalties.

## Traces of Revision

Some surviving manuscripts present a given topic with both an older provision and a newer one, in the form "formerly it was thus, but now it shall be done this way." This is valuable evidence that the Hittite Laws were not fixed once and for all, but a "living code" continuously revised over time.
