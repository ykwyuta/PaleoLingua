## What is transliteration?

"Transliteration" is the process of converting each cuneiform sign impressed on a clay tablet into a corresponding Latin-alphabet representation of the sound or word it stands for. In Assyriology, a transliteration is the first step from a photograph or hand-copy of a tablet toward grammatical analysis and translation. All the example sentences on this site are presented in this transliterated form.

Scholarly publications conventionally set syllabically-spelled words in italics (e.g. *šarrum*), but for readability this site uses plain, non-italic monospace text throughout (e.g. `šarrum`).

## Special letters

Akkadian transliteration uses several letters not found in the English alphabet.

| Letter | Approximate value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| š | Close to English *sh* | Also used in Sumerian transliteration |
| ṣ | An "emphatic" s (with pharyngealization/glottalization) | One of the Semitic "emphatic" consonants |
| ṭ | An emphatic t | Same series as ṣ |
| ḫ | A voiceless fricative made further back in the throat (like German *ch*) | The same symbol used for Hittite ḫ |
| ʾ | A glottal stop (aleph) | Usually appears only adjacent to a vowel |

## Long vowels: macron vs. circumflex

Akkadian distinguishes short and long vowels. Transliteration marks long vowels with a diacritic over the vowel letter.

| Mark | Meaning | Source of the length |
|---|---|---|
| ā, ī, ū, ē (macron) | A vowel that is inherently, etymologically or morphologically, long | Length built into the root or the inflectional pattern |
| â, î, û (circumflex) | A long vowel produced by the contraction of two adjacent vowels | Two vowels merging into one |

For a macron example, take `mātum` "land, country." Its *ā* is etymologically long, and tablets often spell it "plene" (with a doubled vowel sign) to make that length visible.

<p class="cuneiform">𒈠 𒀀 𒌈</p>

```
ma-a-tum
mātum "land, country" (fem. nom. sg., the doubled a-sign marks the long vowel)
```

A circumflex, by contrast, arises from contraction. The G-stem preterite `iqbi` "he said" (root √QBʾ), when it takes the subordinating marker *-u* used in relative and other subordinate clauses, contracts its final *-i* with the following *-u* into a long *û*.

<p class="cuneiform">𒄿 𒅅 𒁍 𒌑</p>

```
i-iq-bu-u2
iqbû "that he said" (subordinate/subjunctive form: i- (3sg) + qbi (root) + -u (subordinating marker), contracted)
```

## Hyphens: dividing signs within a word

When a word is spelled out sign-by-sign in "broken" (syllabic) spelling, a hyphen separates the individual syllabic signs. The hyphens in `ma-a-tum` and `i-iq-bu-u2` above already follow this rule. The example below spells `šarrum` "king" syllabically.

<p class="cuneiform">𒊭 𒅈 𒊒 𒌝</p>

```
ša-ar-ru-um
šarrum "king" (masc. nom. sg.)
```

## Subscript index numbers: distinguishing homophonous signs

Because the cuneiform script was borrowed from Sumerian, several different signs can represent the same syllable (homophony). Transliteration distinguishes them with subscript index numbers. For example, the syllable "du" can be written with any of several different signs, conventionally numbered `du`, `du₂`, `du₃`, and so on.

| Transliteration | Cuneiform | Sign name |
|---|---|---|
| du | 𒁺 | DU |
| du₂ | 𒌅 | TU |
| du₃ | 𒆕 | KAK |

The index number is purely a scholarly bookkeeping device marking which glyph is used — it carries no information about pronunciation itself, and can be ignored when reading the word aloud.

## Uppercase: Sumerograms (logograms)

Akkadian scribes frequently wrote an entire word using a single Sumerian-derived logogram (a "Sumerogram"). Such Sumerian-origin spellings are conventionally written entirely in uppercase in transliteration. The example below spells the same word `šarrum` "king" not syllabically but with a logogram.

<p class="cuneiform">𒈗</p>

```
LUGAL
šarrum "king," written with a logogram (originally Sumerian lugal "king")
```

## Superscript: determinatives

A determinative is a silent classifier sign, never itself pronounced, that tells the reader what semantic category the adjacent word belongs to (deity, place, wooden object, profession, and so on). In transliteration a determinative is written as a superscript. The example below writes the name of the sun-god Šamaš — one of this site's two mascot characters — with the superscript determinative `d` that marks divine names.

<p class="cuneiform">𒀭𒌓</p>

```
dUTU
Šamaš (the sun-god) — d is a silent divine-name determinative, UTU is the logogram for "sun"
```

## Broken (syllabic) spelling alongside Sumerograms

The same word can be spelled differently depending on how much grammatical information the scribe wants to make explicit. The logogram `LUGAL` alone tells the reader the word is "king," but not its case or number. Scribes could add a "phonetic complement" — a syllabic sign appended after the logogram — to spell out a grammatical ending.

<p class="cuneiform">𒈗𒌝</p>

```
LUGAL-um
šarrum "king" (LUGAL is the logogram; -um is a phonetic complement spelling out the nominative ending)
```

A fully syllabic ("broken") spelling like the `ša-ar-ru-um` seen earlier, by contrast, spells out every sound down to the case ending `-um`, so its grammatical form is unambiguous from the transliteration alone. This is why grammatical paradigm tables generally prefer syllabic spellings.
