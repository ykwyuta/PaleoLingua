## Memorial inscriptions: a genre of their own

The great majority of surviving runic inscriptions are **memorial inscriptions**, raised in honor of a deceased family member or companion. Sweden alone preserves several thousand runestones, most of them concentrated in the 11th century, during the spread of Christianity. Their content is remarkably formulaic — once you learn the basic structure, you can read a surprising amount of an unfamiliar inscription at first sight.

## The basic formula

A typical memorial inscription follows this structure:

```
X   reisti  stein  {at / eftir}  Y,  {relation}  sinn/sína
```

| Element | Role |
|---|---|
| X (nominative) | the person who commissioned the stone |
| reisti | 3rd sg. past tense of *reisa* ("to raise") |
| stein | "stone" (accusative) |
| at / eftir | preposition meaning "in memory of" (governs dative or accusative) |
| Y | the name of the deceased |
| relation + sinn/sína | the relationship term with a reflexive possessive, e.g. "his own father," "his own brother" |

## Reading a typical example

```
Ásmundr  reisti  stein  eftir  Þorstein,  fǫður  sinn.
```

**Word-by-word gloss**

| Ásmundr | reisti | stein | eftir | Þorstein | fǫður | sinn |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ásmundr(NOM) | raised | stone(ACC) | in memory of | Þorsteinn(ACC) | father(ACC) | his own(REFL.POSS) |

**Translation**: "Ásmundr raised this stone in memory of Þorsteinn, his [own] father."

`sinn` ("his own") is a reflexive possessive, marking that the subject of the sentence (here, Ásmundr himself) is the possessor. Rather than a plain "his father" (*hans faðir*), it explicitly signals "the subject's own father" — a typical use of the Indo-European reflexive.

## A causative variant

Inscriptions frequently use the causative auxiliary *láta* ("to have [something] done") to show that the commissioner had someone else do the actual carving.

```
Ásmundr  lét  reisa  stein  eftir  Þorstein.
```

**Gloss**: "Ásmundr had [it] raised — a stone, in memory of Þorsteinn."

`lét` ("had done") is the past tense of *láta*, taking the infinitive `reisa` ("to raise"). This construction, showing a wealthy patron hiring a carver to do the work, is especially common on large, elaborate stones.

## A Christian addition after Christianization

From the 11th century on, many inscriptions add a closing line reflecting Christian influence.

```
Guð hjalpi sál hans.
```

**Gloss**: "May God help his soul" (*guð* = God, *hjalpi* = may [he] help〈subjunctive〉, *sál* = soul(ACC), *hans* = his).

Whether or not this line appears is an important clue for dating an inscription relative to the progress of Christianization.
