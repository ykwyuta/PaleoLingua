## More than just uniliteral signs

The previous article, "[The 24 Uniliteral Signs](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/uniliteral-signs-alphabet/)," covered the 24 signs that each represent a single consonant. But hieroglyphic phonograms also include many **biliteral signs**, each representing a sequence of two consonants, and **triliteral signs**, each representing a sequence of three. These let scribes spell a word more compactly than stringing together uniliteral signs one by one.

## A classic biliteral: nfr ("good, beautiful")

One of the best-known biliteral signs spells nfr, "good, beautiful, perfect, young." A single sign conveys this sequence of consonants (n-f-r; strictly speaking, some Egyptologists classify nfr among the more complex multi-consonant signs rather than a pure biliteral, but it is traditionally cited as the textbook example of a compact phonogram). It also functions as a meaningful word entirely on its own.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓄤</p>

```
nfr
good, beautiful, perfect, young (a single sign representing multiple consonants)
```

## Phonetic complements

When scribes used a biliteral or triliteral sign, they often repeated part of its consonants afterward using a uniliteral sign, purely to help the reader confirm the correct reading. This practice is called a **phonetic complement**. Though technically redundant, it was favored for clarity and visual balance. In nfr's case, the final f and r are frequently echoed with uniliteral signs.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓄤𓆑𓂋</p>

```
nfr-f-r
good, beautiful (biliteral nfr + phonetic complements f, r)
```

Here the sounds f and r are already contained within the sign 𓄤 itself, yet the uniliteral signs 𓆑 (f) and 𓂋 (r) are added again as reinforcement. It's a bit like how furigana in Japanese confirms the reading of a kanji character that could otherwise be ambiguous.

## Triliteral signs (or triliteral roots spelled with multiple signs)

Dedicated signs exist for spelling three consonants at once, but many triliteral roots have no such dedicated sign and are instead spelled by combining uniliteral signs. Take mdw, "word, speech, staff" (the phrase mdw-nṯr, "god's words," is itself a well-known ancient term for hieroglyphic writing). Since mdw has no dedicated triliteral sign, it is written with the uniliteral signs m, d, and w in sequence.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓅓𓂧𓅱</p>

```
mdw
word, speech, staff (spelled with uniliteral signs m + d + w)
```

This mix — some roots with a dedicated bi- or triliteral sign, others relying purely on combinations of uniliteral signs — is an important pattern to recognize when reading hieroglyphic texts.
