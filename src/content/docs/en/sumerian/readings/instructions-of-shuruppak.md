## One of the oldest surviving literary works

The *Instructions of Shuruppak* takes the form of a father, Šuruppak, passing down life lessons and practical wisdom to his son Ziusudra, king of Shuruppak (the same Ziusudra later known as the hero of the flood story). The earliest manuscripts go back to the Early Dynastic IIIa period (the Fara period, roughly the 26th century BCE) at Abu Salabikh, making it one of the oldest literary texts known to survive anywhere. Copies continued to be made for centuries afterward, down through the Old Babylonian period.

## A style built on prohibitions

The text is characterized by a stacked series of "do not ~" cautionary clauses. Separate from `nu-`, the ordinary declarative negator covered in [The Negative Prefix nu-](/en/sumerian/grammar/negation-nu/), Sumerian has a dedicated prefix, `na-`, for prohibitions — "do not ~!" (negating a command, i.e., the vetitive).

<p class="cuneiform">𒈾𒆕</p>

```
na-du3
na-du3
PROH-build
"do not build (it)!"
```

`na-` thus occupies a different slot with a different function from `nu-` — it is a negator reserved specifically for commands.

## nu- versus na-

| Prefix | Use | Example | Cuneiform | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `nu-` | Negating a declarative sentence | nu-du3 | 𒉡𒆕 | "did not build" |
| `na-` | Prohibition (negating a command) | na-du3 | 𒈾𒆕 | "do not build!" |

Although both are "negation" in a broad sense, `nu-` (used in statements) and `na-` (used for commands and prohibitions) serve distinct grammatical functions.

## Why this genre of wisdom literature matters

Wisdom texts like the *Instructions of Shuruppak* offer a valuable window into the social norms and everyday ethics of the time — advice such as not carelessly standing surety for someone, or not doing business with someone untrustworthy. Its structure, built from a stack of terse prohibitive clauses, is stylistically close to the proverb collections covered in [Reading Sumerian Proverbs](/en/sumerian/readings/sumerian-proverbs/).
