## Saussure's "Prediction"

In 1879, a young Ferdinand de Saussure theorized the existence of a set of unknown phonemes — with no direct trace surviving in any Indo-European language known at the time — in order to explain patterns of vowel alternation (ablaut) in Proto-Indo-European. This later became known as the **"laryngeal theory."** In Saussure's day there was no way to actually verify this hypothetical set of sounds; it was purely a theoretical requirement worked out backward from patterns of vowel alternation.

## The Discovery of Hittite and Direct Evidence for the Laryngeals

After Bedřich Hrozný identified Hittite as an Indo-European language in 1915, scholars soon noticed that the Hittite consonant **ḫ** was a direct surviving reflex of at least one of the Proto-Indo-European laryngeals (traditionally labeled *h₂). This is remembered as one of the rare, dramatic episodes in the history of linguistics in which a sound predicted purely on theoretical grounds was later confirmed by actual textual evidence.

```
ḫa-a-ša-an-za
bear/give birth (root ḫaš-) - participle ending
"(one who has been) born"
```

The root ḫaš- "to give birth" is thought to go back to the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂ens- (or a similar reconstruction), and it is frequently cited as a prime example showing that ḫ reflects the PIE laryngeal *h₂.

## The Core of Laryngeal Theory

Laryngeal theory reconstructs three (or more) laryngeal phonemes for Proto-Indo-European, conventionally written *h₁, *h₂, and *h₃, and holds that they affected the color of adjacent vowels (*h₂, for instance, is thought to have colored a vowel toward /a/, and *h₃ toward /o/) as well as vowel length. In most Indo-European languages these laryngeals were lost, leaving only traces in vowel quality and length, but in Hittite (and other Anatolian languages), at least some laryngeals continued to be written directly as consonants.

## Significance in the History of Linguistics

The discovery of ḫ in Hittite was widely received as powerful confirmation of the methodology of comparative reconstruction (both internal and comparative reconstruction) in historical linguistics. A sound predicted on purely theoretical grounds, later found in an independently discovered body of textual evidence, is often compared to the relationship between theoretical prediction and empirical confirmation in the natural sciences, and is frequently cited in the history of linguistics as a striking example that linguistics is not merely descriptive but capable of producing testable predictions.
