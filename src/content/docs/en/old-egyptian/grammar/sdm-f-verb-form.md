## What sḏm=f means

Anyone studying Old Egyptian grammar quickly runs into the term **sḏm=f**. This is not one specific word but an **Egyptological naming convention** for an entire family of "suffix-conjugation" verb forms: a verb root with a [suffix pronoun](/en/old-egyptian/grammar/suffix-pronouns/) (or a following noun subject) attached directly to mark the subject.

Concretely, the verb root **sḏm** "hear" plus the 3rd person singular masculine suffix pronoun **=f** "he" gives the specific example **sḏm=f** "he hears," and this one example is used as the headword for the entire conjugation pattern.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓋴𓆓𓅓𓆑</p>

```
sḏm=f
hear=he
"he hears" (verb root sḏm "hear" + suffix pronoun =f "he")
```

## Why "hear" is the citation verb

sḏm "hear" is traditionally used in Egyptological teaching as the **paradigm/citation verb** because its root consists of three regular consonants (s, ḏ, m) that behave predictably in conjugation. This mirrors the role "walk" plays as the go-to regular verb in English grammar teaching — and "sḏm=f" itself has become a term of art referring to the suffix conjugation in general.

## Multiple forms hide behind one name

Egyptologists distinguish several sḏm=f forms that differ in function and (reconstructed) vocalization, even though they are often indistinguishable in writing. Examples include:

- **Perfective/indicative use**: a straightforward statement of fact, "he heard / hears"
- **Subjunctive use**: expressing wish, purpose, or possibility, "that he may hear"

Because hieroglyphic writing records only consonants, these different forms are frequently spelled identically as sḏm=f, and must be distinguished from context and syntactic position. Since vowels are never written, fully certain reconstruction of the lost vowel patterns that once distinguished these forms remains a genuinely debated area of Egyptology. The upshot is that "sḏm=f" is best understood not as a single form, but as a convenient umbrella term covering several functionally distinct forms.
