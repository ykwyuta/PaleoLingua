## What Is Vedic Accent?

[Vedic Sanskrit](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/what-is-sanskrit/) had a **pitch accent** system (स्वर *svara*, "sound, tone") that distinguished which syllable of a word was pronounced with a higher (or lower) pitch. Unlike a stress accent system such as English's, this system marks the position of accent within a word through the relative pitch of syllables. Vedic accent was not merely a matter of pronunciation flavor — it was a phonologically significant feature involved in distinguishing word forms and grammatical function.

## Three Types of Accent

The traditional phonetic literature (the Prātiśākhya texts, among others) classifies Vedic accent into three types.

| Accent | Sanskrit term | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Udātta ("raised") | उदात्त *udātta* | The syllable pronounced with high pitch; the basic accented syllable |
| Anudātta ("not raised") | अनुदात्त *anudātta* | The unaccented syllable, pronounced with low pitch |
| Svarita ("sounded") | स्वरित *svarita* | The syllable immediately following an udātta, falling from high to low pitch; largely arises secondarily, often through semivocalization |

Of these, udātta is the true "accented syllable," anudātta is the unaccented syllable, and svarita is best understood as a composite tone derived secondarily from udātta.

## Marking in Vedic Texts

The Sanskrit tradition uses special auxiliary marks in Vedic manuscripts and printed editions to indicate accent. In one representative system (as seen in the Ṛgveda tradition), udātta is left unmarked, anudātta is marked with a horizontal line below the vowel, and svarita is marked with a vertical line above the vowel. The exact conventions vary somewhat by text and by recitational tradition (śākhā), and it is worth noting that they were never fully standardized. This accent notation is an additional layer specific to Vedic texts, not part of the ordinary orthography described in [An Overview of the Devanāgarī Script](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/devanagari-script-overview/).

## Loss in Classical Sanskrit

By the Classical Sanskrit period, this pitch accent system had been lost, and only traces of accentual rules survive embedded within [Pāṇini's](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/what-is-sanskrit/) grammar itself (Pāṇini's own grammar does include rules governing accent, but these are largely prescriptive, aimed at the correct recitation of Vedic texts rather than describing a living feature of Classical speech). Classical Sanskrit, as a spoken and written language, no longer has any phonologically functioning accent distinction. In this sense, Vedic accent can be called "a fossil of sound that survives only within texts."

## Significance for Comparative Linguistics

Vedic accent is an extremely important piece of evidence for comparative Indo-European linguistics.

- **Correspondence with Greek accent**: Ancient Greek also preserved a pitch accent system inherited from Proto-Indo-European. The position of accent in Vedic and in Greek often agrees in etymologically corresponding words, providing strong evidence that both languages' accent systems descend from a shared Proto-Indo-European accentual system.
- **Correspondence with Balto-Slavic accent**: Baltic languages such as Lithuanian also preserve a complex system of tone and accent, and comparison with Vedic accent is one of the key resources used in reconstructing Proto-Indo-European accentuation.
- **The connection to Verner's Law**: **Verner's Law** in Germanic explains irregular consonant voicing that Grimm's Law alone cannot account for, by appealing to the position of accent (stress) at the Proto-Indo-European stage. Since the Germanic languages themselves retain no direct trace of that original accent, evidence from languages that directly preserve accent — Vedic Sanskrit and Greek — played a decisive role in testing and confirming the validity of Verner's Law.

In this way, Vedic accent, closely intertwined with the ablaut phenomenon discussed in [Vowel Gradation (Guṇa and Vṛddhi)](/en/sanskrit/script-and-phonology/vowel-gradation-guna-vrddhi/), remains one of the most important pieces of evidence for reconstructing the Proto-Indo-European sound system.
