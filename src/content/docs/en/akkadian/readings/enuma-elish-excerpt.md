## The Babylonian Creation Epic

**Enūma Eliš** ("When Above," from its opening words) is a seven-tablet Babylonian creation epic that narrates how the god **Marduk** defeats Tiamat, the primeval sea-goddess, creates the ordered world from her body, and is elevated to king of the gods. As with the [Epic of Gilgamesh](/en/akkadian/readings/gilgamesh-akkadian-excerpt/), the work's modern title is simply its opening words — a common Mesopotamian practice for naming literary compositions.

The date of composition is debated, but many scholars associate the epic, or at least a major stage of its editing, with the reign of the Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar I (12th century BCE), who famously recovered the cult statue of Marduk after it had been carried off by Elam. The epic is generally understood as promoting Marduk's status as head of the Babylonian pantheon in tandem with Babylon's political ascendancy, though the precise circumstances of its composition remain a matter of scholarly discussion. Most surviving copies, like those of [the Epic of Gilgamesh](/en/akkadian/readings/gilgamesh-akkadian-excerpt/), come from Ashurbanipal's royal library at Nineveh; Assyrian versions of the text also survive in which the hero role is transferred from Marduk to the Assyrian national god Aššur.

The epic was not merely literature — it also served as a ritual text central to Babylon's New Year **Akitu festival**. Tradition holds that the entire epic was recited by a priest before the cult statue of Marduk during the festival, and scholars generally understand this annual recitation of the creation narrative and Marduk's rise to kingship as a way of renewing and reaffirming both cosmic order and the legitimacy of Babylonian kingship each year.

## The Opening Line: "When Above, the Heavens Had Not Been Named"

Tablet I opens with the following line, describing the primeval state before heaven and earth possessed "names" — that is, before they existed as distinct entities.

<p class="cuneiform">𒂊𒉡𒈠 𒂊𒇺 𒆷 𒈾𒁍𒌑 𒊭𒈠𒈬</p>

```
e-nu-ma    e-liš     la        na-bu-u2   ša-ma-mu
when       above     not       named      heaven(pl.NOM)
"When above, the heavens had not been named"
```

## Word-by-Word Analysis

| Transliteration | Cuneiform | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| e-nu-ma | 𒂊𒉡𒈠 | enu "when" (a variant of inūma) + -ma | when |
| e-liš | 𒂊𒇺 | el- "above" + -iš (adverbial ending) | above, on high |
| la | 𒆷 | negative particle | not |
| na-bu-u₂ | 𒈾𒁍𒌑 | verbal adjective of `nabû`, "to name" (passive sense) | named |
| ša-ma-mu | 𒊭𒈠𒈬 | nominative plural of `šamû`, "heaven" | the heavens |

## Grammatical Notes

- **`enūma` and `inūma`**: Both mean "when" and mark a subordinate clause; `enūma` is simply a vocalic/dialectal variant of the `inūma` seen in [The Prologue to the Code of Hammurabi](/en/akkadian/readings/hammurabi-code-prologue/).
- **The adverbial ending `-iš`**: `eliš`, "above," carries the adverbial case ending `-iš`, which attaches to noun and adjective stems to form adverbs of manner or direction ("like X," "toward X").
- **The verbal adjective `nabû`**: The verb `nabû`, "to name, call," has a weak final root consonant (a glide), which often produces irregular inflected and derived forms. Here it appears as a verbal adjective with passive force, negated by `la` to mean "not (yet) named."
- **The nominative plural `šamāmū`**: `šamû`, "heaven, sky," is frequently used in the plural, taking the nominative plural ending `-ū` to give `šamāmū`. It is the subject of the sentence, paired in the following line with the parallel clause "nor was the earth (below) called by name."
