## Quick facts

Ancient Egyptian was spoken along the Nile Valley for roughly 4,000 years, from the late 4th millennium BCE down to the last stage of the language, Coptic. It has one of the longest continuously attested written histories of any human language, recorded chiefly in hieroglyphs (from Greek "sacred carving").

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Region spoken | Egypt (the Nile Valley) |
| Span of written attestation | c. 3200 BCE to roughly the 5th century CE for hieroglyphs/Demotic; Coptic survived as a spoken vernacular into the early 20th century |
| Stage PaleoLingua focuses on | Middle Egyptian (spoken c. 2100–1600 BCE, then used as a prestige literary/religious language for nearly two more millennia) |
| Genetic affiliation | An independent branch of the Afroasiatic language family (alongside Semitic, Berber, Cushitic, Chadic, etc.) |
| Script | Hieroglyphs, later giving rise to the cursive Hieratic, Demotic, and finally the Coptic alphabet |
| Basic word order | VSO (Verb–Subject–Object, in verbal sentences) |
| Typological profile | Root-and-pattern inflection based on consonantal roots; grammatical gender (masculine/feminine) and number (singular/dual/plural) |

## The five stages of Egyptian

Egyptologists traditionally divide the language's history into five stages, based on grammatical and lexical change.

| Stage | Approximate dates | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Old Egyptian | c. 3200–2100 BCE | The earliest attested stage, seen above all in the Pyramid Texts |
| Middle Egyptian | c. 2100–1600 BCE | "Classical Egyptian" — remained the literary/religious standard for centuries after it ceased to be spoken |
| Late Egyptian | c. 1600–700 BCE | The vernacular of the New Kingdom; major grammatical shifts, including the rise of a definite article |
| Demotic Egyptian | c. 700 BCE–5th century CE | Written in a highly cursive script, also called Demotic |
| Coptic | 1st century CE–present (still used liturgically) | The final stage, written with the Greek alphabet plus extra letters; its vowel notation is a crucial source for reconstructing pronunciation |

## Genetic affiliation: a branch of Afroasiatic

Egyptian is an independent branch of the Afroasiatic language family, alongside Semitic (Akkadian, Hebrew, Arabic, etc.), Berber, Cushitic, and Chadic. Like Semitic languages, it builds words from consonantal roots — a typological resemblance — but Egyptian is not itself a Semitic language; the two are best understood as sister branches descending from a common Afroasiatic ancestor.

## What PaleoLingua covers: mainly Middle Egyptian

Introductory Egyptological grammars (Gardiner, Allen, and others) are built almost entirely around Middle Egyptian. Middle Egyptian was the vernacular of the Middle Kingdom (c. 2100–1600 BCE), but it went on to serve as the standard literary and monumental language for religious and official texts long after it ceased to be spoken — much as Latin remained a learned language in Europe for centuries after Rome fell. Unless otherwise noted, PaleoLingua's Old Egyptian articles focus on Middle Egyptian grammar, vocabulary, and source texts.

## The surviving corpus

Surviving hieroglyphic material falls broadly into several categories: the Pyramid Texts carved on the interior walls of pyramids (the oldest large-scale corpus of religious writing), the Coffin Texts inscribed on coffins, the Book of the Dead and related funerary literature from the New Kingdom onward, narrative literature such as the Tale of Sinuhe and the Westcar Papyrus, wisdom literature such as the Instruction of Ptahhotep, and inscriptional material — tomb autobiographies, royal inscriptions, and boundary stelae. These texts survive on stone monuments, tomb walls, and papyri, written either in hieroglyphs or in the cursive hieratic script derived from them.
