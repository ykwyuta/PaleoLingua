## No vowels survive in the script

As explained in "[The Hieroglyphic Writing System](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/hieroglyphic-writing-system/)" and "[Transliteration Conventions](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/transliteration-conventions/)," hieroglyphic phonograms record only consonants (plus the semi-vowels j and w); vowels are, as a rule, never written. This means the hieroglyphic spelling of a word alone cannot tell us what vowels ancient Egyptians actually spoke. This article looks at how modern scholars partially reconstruct those lost vowels, and at the conventional pronunciation used for teaching purposes.

## Clue 1: Coptic

Coptic, the final stage of Egyptian, is written with the Greek alphabet plus extra letters, and it **does write vowels explicitly**. Because Coptic developed directly from earlier stages of the language, including Middle Egyptian, its vowel patterns provide a valuable clue for extrapolating backward toward the vowels of earlier stages. That said, Coptic is itself the youngest stage of Egyptian, separated from Middle Egyptian by many centuries of sound change, so this extrapolation has real limits.

## Clue 2: Akkadian and Greek transcriptions

Foreign-language sources sometimes transcribed Egyptian names and loanwords in their own scripts. Akkadian cuneiform documents (such as diplomatic correspondence) and Greek sources both write vowels explicitly, so examining how they render Egyptian proper names offers indirect evidence for Egyptian vowel sounds at the time. How New Kingdom pharaohs' names were spelled in Akkadian diplomatic texts, for instance, is an important source for vowel reconstruction.

## Clue 3: Comparative Afroasiatic evidence

Egyptian is a branch of the Afroasiatic language family, alongside Semitic, Berber, Cushitic, and Chadic (see "[What Is Old Egyptian?](/en/old-egyptian/script-and-phonology/what-is-old-egyptian/)"). Comparing the vowel patterns of corresponding roots across these related languages offers another avenue for estimating Egyptian vowels — though this remains comparative inference rather than direct evidence.

## The conventional "e"-insertion reading

Even combining all these clues, scholars cannot fully reconstruct the vowels of every Egyptian word with certainty. For this reason, modern Egyptological textbooks and teaching materials widely adopt a convention: inserting a conventional "e" sound between consonants **purely to make words pronounceable**. Under this convention, nfr, "good, beautiful," is conventionally read "nefer."

**This "nefer" pronunciation is not a reconstruction of the actual ancient sound — it is simply a modern convenience for learners to pronounce words aloud.** Every katakana reading given in this site's Japanese-language articles follows this same convention, and it's worth repeating that point in every article that uses it.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓄤</p>

```
nfr
conventionally read "nefer" (a modern teaching convention, not the reconstructed ancient pronunciation)
good, beautiful, perfect
```

## The debate over ꜣ and ꜥ

The transliteration letters ꜣ (alef) and ꜥ (ayin) are, by Egyptological convention, frequently pronounced as something close to "a." But this is purely a pronunciation convenience — very few scholars believe these letters actually represented vowels in antiquity. The commonly held view is that ꜣ and ꜥ were **consonants**, similar to the Semitic alef (ʾ, a glottal stop) and ayin (ʿ, a voiced pharyngeal fricative) — that is, glottal or pharyngeal sounds articulated deep in the throat. In other words, they were likely not vowels at all, but distinctive consonants of a kind that don't exist in most European languages. The convention of reading them as "a" persists precisely because European languages lack these throat consonants, making a vowel-like reading the practical choice for learners.
