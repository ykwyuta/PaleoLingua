## The Challenge of Inferring Phonology Through Cuneiform

Since no one can hear how Hittite speakers actually pronounced their language, its phoneme inventory can only be inferred indirectly — from spelling patterns in borrowed cuneiform, from how later loanwords and place names were transmitted, and from comparison with related Anatolian and Indo-European languages. Cuneiform was originally designed for Sumerian and Akkadian, and there is no guarantee it captures Hittite phonology with full precision. The inventory below therefore represents the **current scholarly consensus**, with debate continuing over various details.

## Consonant Inventory

| Place of articulation | Stops | Fricatives | Nasals | Liquids | Glides |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bilabial | p, b | — | m | — | w |
| Dental | t, d | s | n | l, r | — |
| Velar | k, g | — | — | — | y |
| Uvular/laryngeal | — | ḫ | — | — | — |

The pairs p/b, t/d, and k/g in the stop row are a conventional way of representing a traditional voiced/voiceless contrast, but in practice the cuneiform spelling is thought to have distinguished a "weak" (lenis) from a "strong" (fortis) consonant by writing the consonant sign **once versus twice** (single versus geminate spelling). This phenomenon is called "consonant gradation"; see [Consonant Gradation (Fortis/Lenis)](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/consonant-gradation-fortis-lenis/) for details.

## The Laryngeal ḫ

Hittite has a phoneme **ḫ**, reconstructed as a uvular or velar fricative. It is thought to be a surviving reflex of one of the "laryngeals" reconstructed for Proto-Indo-European, and as detailed in [Laryngeals in Hittite](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/laryngeals-in-hittite/), it had a major impact on comparative Indo-European linguistics.

## Vowel Inventory

| Vowel | Example |
|---|---|
| a | a-ap-pa "back" |
| e | e-eš-zi "he/she is" |
| i | i-ya-zi "he/she makes" |
| u | ku-iš "who" |

Hittite is attested with four vowels — a, e, i, u — and no independent phoneme corresponding to "o" is attested (it occasionally shows up in loanwords). Vowel length is thought to have been phonemically distinctive, and long vowels are often marked through "plene writing," in which the same vowel sign is written twice in a row. See [Plene Writing and Vowel Length](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/plene-writing-vowel-length/) for more.

## An Overview of Syllable Structure

The basic Hittite syllable has a single consonant plus a single vowel as its nucleus, optionally flanked by further consonants. For the detailed structure and its constraints, see [Syllable Structure](/en/hittite/script-and-phonology/syllable-structure-hittite/).
