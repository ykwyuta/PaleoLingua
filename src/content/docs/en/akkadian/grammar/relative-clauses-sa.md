## Two Jobs for ša

Akkadian `ša` is an **invariable particle** — it never inflects for case, gender, or number — yet it is one of the hardest-working words in the grammar. It has two main functions:

1. Introducing a **relative clause**, meaning "who/which/that (did something)."
2. Linking two nouns as a **genitive periphrasis** ("of"), serving as an alternative to the [construct state](/en/akkadian/grammar/construct-state/) for expressing possession or belonging.

## Genitive Periphrasis: An Alternative to the Construct State

"The house of the king" can be expressed with the construct state as `bīt šarrim` (house.CONSTRUCT + king.GEN), or periphrastically with `ša`.

<p class="cuneiform">𒁉𒌈 𒊭 𒊭𒅈𒊑𒅎</p>

```
bītum   ša   šarrim
house-NOM   of   king-GEN
"the house of the king"
```

In the construct-state phrase `bīt šarrim`, the first noun (`bītum`) takes a special "construct" form that drops its mimation (`-um`). With `ša`, by contrast, the first noun keeps its full, ordinary form (`bītum`). `ša` functions here like a free-standing word meaning "of," linking two full noun phrases. For the mechanics of the construct state and when each strategy is preferred, see [The Construct State](/en/akkadian/grammar/construct-state/).

## ša as a Relative Pronoun: Introducing Relative Clauses

The other, more central function of `ša` is as a **relative-clause marker**, attaching an entire clause (including a verb) to a noun.

<p class="cuneiform">𒀀𒉿𒈝 𒊭 𒁉𒋫𒄠 𒄿𒁍𒋗</p>

```
awīlum   ša   bītam    īpušu
man-NOM   who   house-ACC  he.built(subordinate)
"the man who built the house"
```

Here `ša` links "man" (`awīlum`) to the relative clause "built the house" (`bītam īpušu`). Note that the verb inside the relative clause, `īpušu` ("he built"), carries an ending `-u` not found on verbs in independent main clauses — this is the subordinate-clause marker (the "subordinative"), distinct from the finite verb forms of ordinary main clauses discussed in [Akkadian Word Order (SOV)](/en/akkadian/grammar/akkadian-word-order-sov/).

## The Significance of Invariability

The defining feature of `ša` is that it **never changes shape**, regardless of the gender, number, or case of the noun it relates to. Whether the antecedent is masculine or feminine, singular or plural, in the nominative or the genitive or the accusative, the same `ša` is used every time.

This stands out sharply against other Semitic languages. Classical Arabic's relative pronoun, for instance, inflects for the antecedent's gender and number: `alladhī` (m.sg.), `allatī` (f.sg.), `alladhīna` (m.pl.), and so on. Biblical Hebrew likewise has, in certain constructions, relative elements that vary with gender and number. For readers who know either of these languages, the sheer invariability of Akkadian `ša` may come as a small surprise. Akkadian, in effect, radically simplifies its relative-clause marker into a single unchanging particle, relying on context and word order — rather than inflection — to keep the relationship with the antecedent clear.
