## What Are Borrowed Logograms?

Open any Hittite text and you will quickly notice words written in capital letters (by scholarly transliteration convention, small caps or full caps). These are not spelled out in native Hittite syllables at all — they are words from other languages reused wholesale as logograms (word-signs that represent a whole word with a single symbol).

- **Sumerogram**: a Sumerian word borrowed and used as a logogram, shown in capital letters in transliteration. Examples: LUGAL "king," DINGIR "god," URU "city."
- **Akkadogram**: an Akkadian word form (often an inflected form) borrowed and used the same way, conventionally shown in italic capitals. Examples: *ANA* "to, for" (a preposition), *ŠA* "of" (a prepositional expression marking the genitive).

Neither is meant to be read aloud "in Sumerian" or "in Akkadian." Rather, both scribe and reader are thought to have mentally converted the logogram into the corresponding Hittite word as they wrote or read — much like reading "Mr." as "mister" in English, or reading the numeral "3" as "three" rather than sounding out the digit itself.

## A Concrete Example

```
LUGAL-uš URU-ri
king (Sumerogram)-NOM city (Sumerogram)-DIR
"the king, to the city"
```

The typical pattern is to attach only the Hittite case ending to a Sumerogram stem, spelled out in syllabic signs — a so-called "phonetic complement." This ending also serves as a clue telling the reader which Hittite case form the Sumerogram should be read as.

## Why Were They Used So Heavily?

Several practical reasons are thought to lie behind the heavy use of Sumerograms and Akkadograms:

- **Scribal efficiency**: writing common, frequent words (king, god, city, house, sheep, etc.) with a single logogram saves the effort of spelling them out in full.
- **A shared, international scribal tradition**: Sumerograms functioned almost like standardized international abbreviations across the entire cuneiform-writing world (Akkadian, Hurrian, Ugaritic, and more), and Hittite scribes simply inherited this convention.
- **Terminological clarity**: fixing divine names, place names, and titles as Sumerograms helped avoid ambiguity in complex, multilingual documents.

## When the Pronunciation Cannot Be Recovered

A weakness of Sumerograms and Akkadograms is that the underlying native Hittite word and its pronunciation are not always recoverable. If a given word is attested only as a Sumerogram, with no syllabically spelled-out example ever found, its actual Hittite pronunciation may remain genuinely unknown to this day. In such cases scholars typically limit themselves to informed guesses based on cognates in related Indo-European or Anatolian languages, rather than proposing a definitive reconstruction.
