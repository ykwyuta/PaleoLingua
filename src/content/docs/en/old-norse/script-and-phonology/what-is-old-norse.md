## Quick facts

Old Norse (natively just *dǫnsk tunga*, "the Danish tongue," or *norrœnt mál*) is a North Germanic language spoken across Scandinavia and its overseas settlements — Iceland, the Faroe Islands, Greenland, and for a time Novgorod and Normandy — from roughly the 8th to the 14th century CE. It spread through the trade, raiding, and settlement of the Viking Age (c. 793–1066) and is the language behind the Poetic Edda and the saga literature.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Region spoken | Scandinavia, Iceland, the Faroe Islands, and scattered colonies (Novgorod, Normandy, Dublin, etc.) |
| Period | c. 8th–14th century CE (after which it splits into the various Old/Middle Scandinavian languages) |
| Genetic affiliation | Indo-European > Germanic > North Germanic (subdivided into Old East Norse and Old West Norse) |
| Script | Runic alphabet (futhark); Latin script alongside it from the 13th century onward |
| Basic word order | Broadly SVO, with a verb-second (V2) constraint |
| Typological profile | Inflectional; four noun cases, strong/weak noun and verb classes, grammatical gender (masc./fem./neut.) |
| Modern descendants | Icelandic, Faroese (from Old West Norse); Norwegian, Swedish, Danish (from Old East Norse) |

## Dialects and split

Old Norse is broadly divided into **Old West Norse** (Norway and its Atlantic colonies, above all Iceland and the Faroes) and **Old East Norse** (Denmark and Sweden). Most of the surviving literature — the Poetic Edda and the sagas in particular — is in Old West Norse, specifically the form recorded in Iceland, commonly called Old Icelandic. This is also the normalized form that this site treats as "Old Norse" by default.

## From runes to the Latin alphabet

The script used to record Old Norse changed over time. Throughout the Viking Age, everyday writing used **runes**, carved into stone, wood, bone, and metal. The runic alphabet had developed from the common North Germanic **Elder Futhark** (24 characters) into the simplified, 16-character **Younger Futhark** used during the Viking Age itself, tracking sound changes in the spoken language. From the 11th century on, Christianization brought the Latin alphabet, and it was in Latin script — on Icelandic vellum manuscripts — that the great literary works, the Poetic Edda and the sagas, were eventually written down. This site covers the runic writing system in the script-and-phonology articles, while grammar and reading articles mostly use the normalized Latin-script spelling of standard Old Norse.

## The surviving corpus

The corpus splits broadly into two strands. One is the **primary, inscriptional evidence**: several thousand runestones, wooden sticks, and other objects bearing memorial inscriptions, ownership marks, and charms. The other is the **literary corpus**, written down on Icelandic vellum manuscripts from the 13th century onward: the *Poetic Edda*, a collection of mythological and heroic poems; the *Prose Edda*, Snorri Sturluson's handbook of poetics and mythology; and a large body of saga literature, including *Njáls saga* and many other family sagas and kings' sagas.
