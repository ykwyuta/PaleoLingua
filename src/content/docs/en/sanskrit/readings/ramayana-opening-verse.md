## The Poet Vālmīki and the Legend of the "First Śloka"

The **Rāmāyaṇa** ("The Journey of Rāma") tells the life of Prince Rāma of Kosala — his exile, the abduction of his wife Sītā, his expedition to the island of Laṅkā aided by the monkey-king Hanumān and others, and Sītā's rescue — and stands alongside the Mahābhārata as one of India's two great epics. It comprises seven books (*kāṇḍas*) and roughly 24,000 verses, and is traditionally attributed to the sage **Vālmīki**. Its dating remains debated, but a widely held view places the core books (2 through 6) between roughly 400 BCE and the turn of the era, with Book 1 (Bāla Kāṇḍa) and Book 7 (Uttara Kāṇḍa) — generally considered later additions — composed somewhat afterward.

Book 1 of the Rāmāyaṇa contains a famous origin myth in which Vālmīki himself narrates how he came to compose the epic. One day, walking along a riverbank, the sage Vālmīki sees a pair of affectionate *krauñca* birds (variously identified as a type of curlew or a species of crane). Moments later, a hunter (*niṣāda*) shoots the male bird dead with an arrow. Hearing the anguished cry of the bereaved female, Vālmīki is overcome with deep sorrow (*śoka*) and, without premeditation, utters a curse against the hunter. Tradition holds that these words spontaneously took the exact metrical shape of a *śloka* — the standard verse form of Sanskrit poetry — and that this moment marks both the origin of the śloka meter and the very first line of the Rāmāyaṇa itself.

## The Text

```
मा निषाद प्रतिष्ठां त्वमगमः शाश्वतीः समाः ।
यत्क्रौञ्चमिथुनादेकमवधीः काममोहितम् ॥

mā niṣāda pratiṣṭhāṃ tvam agamaḥ śāśvatīḥ samāḥ |
yat krauñcamithunād ekam avadhīḥ kāmamohitam ||
```

**Word-by-word gloss**

| Devanāgarī | IAST | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| मा | mā | prohibitive particle | may... not |
| निषाद | niṣāda | Niṣāda (a term for a hunter/forest-dwelling people), vocative sg. | O hunter (Niṣāda) |
| प्रतिष्ठाम् | pratiṣṭhām | derived noun "standing/renown" from prati-√sthā "to stand," acc. sg. | stability/renown (acc.) |
| त्वम् | tvam | 2nd-person pronoun, nom. sg. | you |
| अगमः | agamaḥ | √gam "to go," 2sg. aorist (with prohibitive mā) | may you not attain |
| शाश्वतीः समाः | śāśvatīḥ samāḥ | śāśvatī "eternal" + samā "year," fem. acc. pl. | for endless years |
| यत् | yat | relative particle "because" | because |
| क्रौञ्चमिथुनात् | krauñca-mithunāt | compound of krauñca "krauñca bird" + mithuna "pair," ablative sg. | from the pair of krauñca birds |
| एकम् | ekam | eka "one," acc. sg. | one (of them) |
| अवधीः | avadhīḥ | √han "to kill," 2sg. aorist | you killed |
| काममोहितम् | kāma-mohitam | compound of kāma "desire/love" + mohita "bewildered/overcome," acc. sg. | (the bird) overcome with love |

**Translation:** "O hunter, may you never find lasting peace (stability) for endless years, since you killed one of a pair of krauñca birds while it was overcome with love."

## The Origin of the Śloka Meter

What makes this passage symbolically important is not just its content but **its metrical form**. According to tradition, the curse Vālmīki uttered spontaneously took the shape of the **śloka** — eight syllables per *pāda* (verse-quarter), four *pādas* per verse — which became the standard meter of later Classical Sanskrit poetry. Part of the charm of this origin myth lies in the wordplay linking sorrow (*śoka*) and verse form (*śloka*). It bears noting, however, that this is a self-referential origin legend told within the epic itself, not historical evidence for the actual origins of the śloka meter.

## The Significance of the Curse Framework

It is also significant that this verse takes the form of a **curse (śāpa)**, rather than a simple expression of grief. Scholars often read this scene as a symbolic prologue anticipating the central themes of the Rāmāyaṇa as a whole — righteous outrage at unjust violence, and retribution for departures from proper conduct (dharma). On this reading, the outrage at a hunter striking down a defenseless creature in the midst of tenderness foreshadows, in the epic's very first line, the theme of righteous indignation against injustice that runs throughout the narrative.
