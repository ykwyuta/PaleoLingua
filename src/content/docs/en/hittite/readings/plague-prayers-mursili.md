## A Plague That Lasted Twenty Years

The **Plague Prayers of Muršili II** are a series of prayers the Hittite king Muršili II (late 14th century BCE) offered to the gods to end a long-running plague that had afflicted Hatti since the reign of his father, Šuppiluliuma I. The plague is said to have claimed Šuppiluliuma I's own life, and Muršili repeatedly sought oracles to determine the cause of this calamity.

## Two Causes Revealed by Oracle

According to the prayers, the oracles pointed to two main causes of the plague.

1. **Violation of a treaty with Egypt**: prisoners brought back from Egypt in the time of Muršili's father, Šuppiluliuma I, were said to have brought the plague with them, and this was interpreted as having provoked divine wrath over the oath sworn with Egypt (the Kurustama Treaty).
2. **A neglected vow of offerings**: the previous king, Tudḫaliya, had failed to fulfill a vow of offerings made to the god of the Mala River (a tributary of the Euphrates), which was held to have angered the gods.

## The Structure of "The Son Bears the Father's Guilt"

A key feature of these prayers is that, even though Muršili did not personally commit these transgressions, the text follows a structure in which he **takes on his forefathers' guilt in his own generation and apologizes to the gods for it**.

Below is a simplified example sentence, for learning purposes, conveying the gist of this confession (note that this is not a verbatim quotation of a specific line, but an illustration of a confessional pattern found in the prayers).

```
at-tar-aš-ma-mu wa-aš-túl ki-ša-at
father's-but-to.me sin become-3SG.PRET.MP
"but my father's sin became mine"

nu-mu DINGIR.MEŠ-eš ka-ru-ú-i-li-uš iš-ta-ma-aš-ker
and-to.me gods ancient listen-3PL.PRET
"and the ancient gods gave ear to me"
```

## A Personal Confession of Faith

Muršili's plague prayers have drawn considerable scholarly attention not merely as a public ritual text but for their introspective tone, tinged with the king's personal anguish and confusion. Muršili directly puts to the gods the question of why he, who committed no wrong himself, must suffer so long — a candor considered unusual among ancient Near Eastern religious literature, and valued as a personal outpouring of faith.
