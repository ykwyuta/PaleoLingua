## Singular, dual, and plural

The most striking feature of Old Norse personal pronouns is that the 1st and 2nd persons have dedicated **dual number** forms. The dual marks "exactly two" (people or things), distinct from both the singular (one) and the plural (three or more). This archaic feature has been lost in modern English, German, and even modern Icelandic — a case where Old Norse still preserves a genuine remnant of Proto-Indo-European grammar.

## First-person pronouns

| Case | Singular ("I") | Dual ("we two") | Plural ("we," 3+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | ek | vit | vér |
| Accusative | mik | okkr | oss |
| Genitive | mín | okkar | vár |
| Dative | mér | okkr | oss |

## Second-person pronouns

| Case | Singular ("you") | Dual ("you two") | Plural ("you," 3+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | þú | it | ér |
| Accusative | þik | ykkr | yðr |
| Genitive | þín | ykkar | yðar |
| Dative | þér | ykkr | yðr |

## Third-person pronouns (singular, by gender)

The third person has no dual form — only singular and plural — and the singular varies by grammatical gender (masculine/feminine/neuter).

| Case | Masculine ("he") | Feminine ("she") | Neuter ("it") |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | hann | hon | þat |
| Accusative | hann | hana | þat |
| Genitive | hans | hennar | þess |
| Dative | honum | henni | því |

The third-person plural takes forms like *þeir* regardless of the actual gender mix of the group (the masculine form is often used by default).

## The dual in action

```
vit  förum   heim
vit  för-um  heim
we.two(NOM) go-1PL.PRES home
"The two of us are going home."
```

If the group in question had three or more members, the subject would be *vér* (plural), not *vit*. The dual was not just "part of the plural" — it was a strictly maintained, independent grammatical category.
