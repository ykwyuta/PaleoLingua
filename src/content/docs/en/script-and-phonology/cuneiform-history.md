## From accounting tokens to pictographs

Cuneiform's roots are usually traced back to small clay "tokens" used across the ancient Near East from around 8000 BCE to keep track of goods — a distinct token shape for a sheep, a jar of oil, and so on. By the very end of the 4th millennium BCE (the Late Uruk period, roughly 3400–3200 BCE), scribes in the southern Mesopotamian city of Uruk began recording this same information as pictographs pressed and drawn into clay with a stylus. These are the earliest known written documents.

## From pictographs to wedges

The earliest script (so-called "proto-cuneiform") consisted of pictographs that visually resembled the objects they represented. But clay as a writing medium made curved lines awkward to produce, while pressing a stylus into the surface to make straight impressions was fast and efficient. Signs were gradually simplified and abstracted as a result. By around 2600 BCE (the Fara period), signs had lost almost all pictorial resemblance to their origins and consisted of combinations of wedge-shaped strokes — giving the script its modern name, "cuneiform," from Latin *cuneus*, "wedge."

## From word-signs to syllables

At first, each sign stood for a whole word (a logogram). This made it impossible to write personal names or grammatical elements such as suffixes. Scribes solved this by borrowing a sign's *sound* rather than its meaning, using it to spell out a syllable instead of a word. This gave cuneiform its lasting character as a mixed logographic-syllabic system, capable of recording the complex, agglutinative grammar of Sumerian.

## Spread to other languages

Although cuneiform was developed for Sumerian, it was subsequently borrowed and adapted to write a range of unrelated languages, including Akkadian (Babylonian and Assyrian), Eblaite, Elamite, Hurrian, Urartian, and Hittite (along with other Anatolian languages). Its use for Akkadian in particular lasted for millennia, and it is thought to have influenced the much simpler, largely alphabetic-syllabic cuneiform script later devised for Old Persian.
