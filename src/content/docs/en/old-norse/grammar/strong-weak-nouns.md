## What "strong" and "weak" mean here

Old Norse nouns (like those of the other Germanic languages) fall into two broad declension types, **strong** and **weak**, based on the old stem-forming suffix that once sat between the root and the case ending. Strong nouns show relatively distinct endings across case and number, with the case/number distinctions clearly visible; weak nouns descend from stems ending in *n* (n-stems), and tend to reuse the same or similar endings (*-a, -i, -u*, etc.) across several cases. The strong/weak split cuts across all three genders — masculine, feminine, and neuter.

## Masculine comparison: armr (strong) vs. goði (weak)

*armr* ("arm") is a strong a-stem noun; *goði* ("chieftain-priest") is a weak n-stem noun.

| Case | armr (strong) sg. | armr pl. | goði (weak) sg. | goði pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | armr | armar | goði | goðar |
| Accusative | arm | arma | goða | goða |
| Genitive | arms | arma | goða | goða |
| Dative | armi | örmum | goða | goðum |

Notice that the weak noun *goði* has the same form, *goða*, for accusative, genitive, and dative singular alike — weak nouns generally show fewer distinct forms than strong nouns.

## Feminine comparison: gjǫf (strong) vs. saga (weak)

The same contrast holds for feminine nouns. *gjǫf* ("gift") is a strong ō-stem noun; *saga* ("story," "saga") is a weak n-stem noun.

| Case | gjǫf (strong) sg. | gjǫf pl. | saga (weak) sg. | saga pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | gjǫf | gjafar | saga | sögur |
| Accusative | gjöf | gjafar | sögu | sögur |
| Genitive | gjafar | gjafa | sögu | sagna |
| Dative | gjöf | gjöfum | sögu | sögum |

It's a nice coincidence that the very word *saga* — "story," the source of the modern English/Japanese term "saga literature" — is itself an example of a weak feminine noun.

## Neuter weak nouns exist, but are rare

Most neuter nouns are strong (a-stems), but a handful of body-part nouns, such as *auga* ("eye"), are weak n-stems. The plural of *auga* is *augu*, and this irregularity persists into modern Icelandic.

## A quick diagnostic

The easiest clue to whether a noun is strong or weak is its **nominative singular ending**.

| Gender | Typical strong nom. sg. ending | Typical weak nom. sg. ending |
|---|---|---|
| Masculine | -r (e.g., armr) | -i (e.g., goði) |
| Feminine | zero ending or -r (e.g., gjǫf) | -a (e.g., saga) |
| Neuter | zero ending (e.g., land) | -a (e.g., auga) |
