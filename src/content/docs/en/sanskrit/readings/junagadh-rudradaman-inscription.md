## The Junagadh Rock Inscription

The **Junagadh rock inscription** is one of several inscriptions carved onto a single large boulder near modern Junagadh, Gujarat, at the foot of Mount Girnar. The same rock also bears the 3rd-century BCE Major Rock Edicts of the Mauryan emperor **Aśoka** (in Prakrit, written in Brāhmī script) and a 5th-century CE inscription of King **Skandagupta**, making the boulder as a whole a microcosm of several centuries of Indian epigraphic history. The inscription discussed here is one commissioned by **Rudradāman I**, a king of the Western Kṣatrapas (a branch of the Śaka/Scythian dynasties), who ruled roughly 130–150 CE, and is dated to around **150 CE** (Śaka year 72). It commemorates Rudradāman's restoration, at his own expense, of the irrigation dam at **Lake Sudarśana** — originally built under the Mauryan governor Puṣyagupta and improved under Aśoka's governor Tuṣāspa — after it was breached by a storm.

## The Inscription's Landmark Place in Scholarship

The Junagadh inscription is especially prized by scholars because it is considered **the earliest surviving lengthy inscription composed in polished, standard Classical Sanskrit prose**. In contrast to Aśoka's rock edicts on the very same boulder, written in Prakrit (a vernacular register), Rudradāman's inscription is composed in a style that draws on elaborate compounds and rhetorical ornamentation characteristic of courtly kāvya (belles-lettres). This is widely regarded as a landmark turning point in the history of Indian epigraphy, marking Sanskrit's establishment as a language of official political documents, beyond its earlier role as primarily a ritual and scholarly language.

## The Text (excerpt)

The inscription as a whole is a long prose composition heavily reliant on elaborate compounds (samāsa), and quoting it in full would require careful verification against primary critical editions. Here we present a phrase concerning Rudradāman's title that is especially frequently cited by historians.

```
स्वयमधिगतमहाक्षत्रपनाम्ना

svayam adhigata-mahākṣatrapa-nāmnā
```

**Word-by-word gloss**

| Devanāgarī | IAST | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| स्वयम् | svayam | adverb "by oneself/personally" | himself |
| अधिगत | adhigata | past participle of adhi-√gam "to attain/acquire" | having acquired |
| महाक्षत्रप | mahā-kṣatrapa | compound of mahā "great" + kṣatrapa "satrap/governor" | Great Satrap (title) |
| नाम्नः | nāmnaḥ | nāman "name/title," instrumental sg. (second half of the compound) | by the title |

(The whole forms an instrumental adjectival compound modifying the person referred to just prior in the text — Rudradāman himself.)

**Translation:** "[By him] who himself acquired the title of Great Satrap (Mahākṣatrapa)."

## The Historical Significance of This Phrase

This short phrase is repeatedly quoted because it condenses **important information about the actual mechanics of succession to the office of Kṣatrapa** during this period. The expression "himself acquired" (*svayam adhigata*) is generally interpreted as emphasizing that Rudradāman gained the office of Great Satrap through his own merit and achievement, rather than through simple hereditary succession from his forebears. The governmental structure of the Western Kṣatrapa dynasty and the principles governing succession to the office of Kṣatrapa continue to be studied alongside numismatic evidence (royal names and dates found on coins), but much remains uncertain, and this phrase retains its value as one of the few contemporary primary sources bearing on the question.

Other parts of the inscription (not quoted here in full; a critical edition by Kielhorn appears in *Epigraphia Indica*, vol. VIII, 1905–06) praise Rudradāman, with elaborate rhetoric, for his mastery of multiple fields of learning including grammar, music, and logic, and for restoring the embankment of Lake Sudarśana without imposing heavy taxation or forced labor on his subjects.
