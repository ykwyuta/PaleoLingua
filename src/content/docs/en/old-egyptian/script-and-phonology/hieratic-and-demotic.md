## Egypt had more than one script

The hieroglyphs examined in earlier articles were the formal, monumental script used on temple walls and stelae. But most everyday administrative documents, literary works, and religious texts were written on papyrus with a reed pen, in a faster, more practical **cursive** hand. This cursive tradition developed through two successive stages: **Hieratic** and, later, **Demotic**.

## Hieratic

Hieratic is a cursive script derived from hieroglyphs by simplifying and running together their forms so they could be written more quickly. It is extremely old, attested from very early in the Old Kingdom, essentially alongside hieroglyphs themselves. The vast majority of practical papyrus texts — administrative records, literary works, religious texts — were written in Hieratic. It was written chiefly right-to-left in horizontal lines; from the New Kingdom onward, administrative and legal texts increasingly used vertical columns as well. Each individual Hieratic sign is a cursive simplification of a hieroglyphic sign, and with training its correspondence to the original hieroglyph can usually be traced.

## Demotic

Around 700 BCE, in the Late Period, an even more cursive and simplified script called **Demotic** emerged, and it came to be used widely for administrative, legal, and literary texts. Demotic was used for administrative, commercial, and legal documents, and later for literary and religious texts as well. Its individual signs became so heavily simplified and distinct that they **can no longer be mapped one-to-one onto hieroglyphic signs** — Demotic functions almost like a separate script in its own right. As a result, the specialized study of Demotic requires somewhat different expertise from hieroglyphic study proper.

## The Rosetta Stone: one decree, three scripts

The relationship among these scripts is famously illustrated by the **Rosetta Stone**. Erected in 196 BCE, this stela records the same Ptolemaic royal decree in three different scripts: **hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek**. It is well known that Jean-François Champollion and others used the Rosetta Stone in the early 19th century to crack the long-lost key to reading hieroglyphs. The Rosetta Stone remains a vivid demonstration both of the relationship among hieroglyphs, Hieratic, and Demotic, and of how valuable it is to have ancient Egyptian recorded alongside a known language like Greek.
