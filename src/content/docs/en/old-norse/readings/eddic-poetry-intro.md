## What is the Poetic Edda?

The *Poetic Edda* (also called the *Elder Edda*) is a collection of Old Norse poems centered on the gods and heroes of Norse mythology. Its single most important surviving manuscript, the *Codex Regius*, was copied in Iceland in the late 13th century and rediscovered in 1643 by the Icelandic bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson. The poems themselves are generally believed to be older, many of them transmitted orally as far back as the 9th–11th centuries.

The origin of the word "Edda" is uncertain — proposed etymologies connect it to a word for "poetics" or to a word meaning "great-grandmother." To distinguish it from Snorri Sturluson's 13th-century handbook of mythology and poetics, the *Prose Edda*, this collection of poems is called the *Poetic Edda* or the *Elder Edda*.

## Structure: mythological and heroic poems

The Poetic Edda splits broadly into two parts.

| Section | Content | Representative poems |
|---|---|---|
| Mythological poems | stories of gods such as Óðinn and Þórr, the creation and end of the cosmos (Ragnarök) | *Vǫluspá* ("The Prophecy of the Seeress"), *Hávamál* ("Sayings of the High One") |
| Heroic poems | stories of heroes such as Sigurðr (the prototype of the Siegfried legend) | *Sigrdrífumál*, *Atlakviða* |

## Meter: alliterative verse

A defining feature of Eddic poetry is that it is built on **alliteration** rather than end rhyme. Each line splits into two half-lines, each carrying stressed syllables ("staves"). The verse is constructed so that the initial consonant of the first stressed syllable ("main stave") in the second half-line matches that of one or two stressed syllables in the first half-line. This alliterative tradition is a hallmark of Germanic poetics, shared with the Old English epic *Beowulf*.

```
Deyr fé,       deyja frændr,
deyr sjalfr it sama;
```

**Gloss**: "Cattle die, kinsmen die, one dies oneself the same [way]."

This line, the opening of stanza 76 of the *Hávamál*, chimes *fé* ("cattle") against *frændr* ("kinsmen") through their shared initial /f/. The mechanics of alliterative verse and a close reading of actual stanzas are covered in the next article, "[Hávamál — Selected Stanzas](/en/old-norse/readings/havamal-selections/)."

## Why read the Poetic Edda?

The Poetic Edda is not only the single most important source for Norse mythology; it is also first-rate material for learning Old Norse grammar, vocabulary, and poetic diction (kennings). As a text that helped set the standard for literary Old Icelandic, it stands alongside the saga literature at the center of Old Norse reading practice.
