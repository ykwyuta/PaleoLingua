Sanskrit (संस्कृत saṃskṛta) is famous among Indo-European languages for preserving an especially rich inflectional system. Nouns, adjectives, and pronouns all decline according to three grammatical categories: **number**, **case**, and **gender**. This article lays out the overall picture as a roadmap for the more detailed articles that follow, such as [The Declension of A-Stem Masculine Nouns](/en/sanskrit/grammar/a-stem-masculine-declension/) and [The Functions of the Case System](/en/sanskrit/grammar/case-system-functions/).

## Three Numbers (vacana)

Like Latin and Greek, Sanskrit retains the **dual** as a full grammatical category alongside the singular and plural — a feature inherited from Proto-Indo-European.

| Number | Sanskrit term | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Singular | एकवचन ekavacana | Refers to one thing |
| Dual | द्विवचन dvivacana | Refers to two things, or naturally paired things (eyes, hands, parents) |
| Plural | बहुवचन bahuvacana | Refers to three or more things |

The dual was lost in the modern Indo-Aryan languages but remained fully productive in Classical Sanskrit. It is especially favored with nouns denoting natural pairs, such as "eyes," "hands," or "parents."

## Eight Cases (vibhakti)

Sanskrit declension tables are traditionally listed in a fixed order of eight cases. This order itself reflects the traditional arrangement used in Pāṇinian grammar (discussed below), and the articles on this site follow the same order.

| Case | Sanskrit term | Core function (overview) |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | प्रथमा prathamā ("first") | Subject of the sentence |
| Vocative | सम्बोधन sambodhana | Direct address |
| Accusative | द्वितीया dvitīyā ("second") | Direct object; goal of motion |
| Instrumental | तृतीया tṛtīyā ("third") | Means, instrument, agent (in passive constructions) |
| Dative | चतुर्थी caturthī ("fourth") | Indirect object; purpose/beneficiary |
| Ablative | पञ्चमी pañcamī ("fifth") | Point of separation; standard of comparison |
| Genitive | षष्ठी ṣaṣṭhī ("sixth") | Possession/belonging |
| Locative | सप्तमी saptamī ("seventh") | Location, time, circumstance |

Some traditional grammars do not count the vocative as an independent case, treating it instead as a variant of the nominative. Because its actual forms often differ from the nominative, however, this site consistently treats it as one of eight cases for pedagogical clarity. The concrete semantic and syntactic functions each case performs are treated with worked examples in [The Functions of the Case System](/en/sanskrit/grammar/case-system-functions/).

## Three Genders (liṅga)

Like Latin and Greek, Sanskrit nouns distinguish **masculine** (पुंलिङ्ग puṃliṅga), **feminine** (स्त्रीलिङ्ग strīliṅga), and **neuter** (नपुंसकलिङ्ग napuṃsakaliṅga). Grammatical gender does not necessarily track biological sex: vṛkṣa- "tree" is masculine, while nadī- "river" is feminine — gender is a lexical property fixed for each word.

Gender strongly affects declension patterns. Even within a single stem class such as the a-stems, masculine and neuter nouns take one set of endings while feminine nouns typically belong to a different stem class altogether (the ā-stems or ī-stems).

## Stem Classes: Vowel Stems and Consonant Stems

Sanskrit noun declension divides broadly into two groups depending on whether the stem ends in a vowel or a consonant.

- **Vowel stems**: stems ending in -a, -ā, -i, -ī, -u, -ū, -ṛ, and the like. Among these, the -a stems (masculine/neuter) and -ā stems (feminine) are by far the most frequent across the lexicon, and are the patterns a beginner should master first. These are covered in detail in [The Declension of A-Stem Masculine Nouns](/en/sanskrit/grammar/a-stem-masculine-declension/).
- **Consonant stems**: stems ending in a consonant, such as -t, -n, -s, -c, or -ah (for example, present participial stems in -ant-, the -an stem rājan- "king," or the -as stem manas- "mind"). Consonant stems show more complex sandhi (euphonic combination) when endings are added, and the stem itself often alternates between strong and weak grades depending on the case.

This two-way split is only a roadmap; in practice each ending vowel or consonant defines its own subclass (i-stems, u-stems, ṛ-stems, n-stems, s-stems, and so on). This site treats the high-frequency a-stem masculine nouns as the representative case in the next article, with other stem classes covered progressively in further articles.

## Relation to Pronouns

Personal pronouns (aham "I," tvam "you") and the demonstrative/third-person pronoun (tad "that, he/she/it") use the same overall case system (eight cases × three numbers) as nouns, but their actual forms diverge substantially from the regular noun paradigms. This is covered in [Pronoun Declension](/en/sanskrit/grammar/pronoun-declension/).
