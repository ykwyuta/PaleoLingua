## Two ways to say "the B of A"

Old Egyptian expresses a possessive or "of" relationship between two nouns in two distinct ways: the **direct genitive**, formed by simple juxtaposition, and the **indirect genitive**, which inserts the genitival adjective **n(y)** "of."

## The direct genitive: simple juxtaposition

In the direct genitive, two nouns are placed side by side in the order "possessed thing + possessor," with nothing between them. It is the simplest and most economical way to express possession.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓊪𓂋𓈖𓍿𓂋</p>

```
pr nṯr
house god
"house of god / temple" (literally: house god)
```

The direct genitive is typically used when the relationship between the two nouns is close or inherent — body parts, family relationships, or fixed institutional pairings like pr nṯr "temple" (literally "house of god").

## The indirect genitive: inserting n(y)

The indirect genitive inserts the genitival adjective **n(y)** (masculine) / **n.t** (feminine) / **n.w** (masculine plural) between the two nouns. This word means "of" and, like any other adjective, agrees in gender and number with the **possessed noun** (the one it follows).

<p class="hieroglyph">𓊪𓂋𓈖𓈖𓍿𓂋</p>

```
pr n(y) nṯr
house of(masc.) god
"the house of the god" (indirect genitive — more explicit/emphatic)
```

Both pr nṯr (direct) and pr n(y) nṯr (indirect) can be translated "house/temple of god," but the indirect genitive is more explicit and foregrounds the possessive relationship more clearly. As a general tendency, the direct genitive is favored for close, idiomatic pairings, while the indirect genitive is used for looser or more emphasized possession.

## n(y) behaves like an ordinary adjective

n(y) is best analyzed not as a free-standing word but as a "genitival adjective" that follows the noun and agrees with it in gender and number — consistent with the noun-adjective word order covered in [Adjectives and Nisbe Adjectives](/en/old-egyptian/grammar/nisbe-adjectives/).
