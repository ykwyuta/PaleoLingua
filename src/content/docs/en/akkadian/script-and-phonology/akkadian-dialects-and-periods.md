## Two Geographic Dialect Groups

Akkadian was never a single, uniform language. It splits into two major dialect groups that developed in the north and south of Mesopotamia.

| Dialect group | Core region | Major cities |
|---|---|---|
| Babylonian | Southern Mesopotamia (Babylonia) | Babylon, Nippur, Ur, Uruk |
| Assyrian | Northern Mesopotamia (Assyria) | Aššur, Nineveh, Kalḫu (Nimrud) |

As noted in [What Is Akkadian?](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/what-is-akkadian/), this north–south split is both a political boundary and a genuine linguistic one, reflected in differences of phonology, vocabulary, and grammar.

## Periods of the Babylonian Dialect Group

| Period | Approximate dates | Representative genres |
|---|---|---|
| Old Akkadian | c. 2350 – c. 2150 BCE | Royal inscriptions and administrative texts of the Akkadian Empire (Sargonic dynasty); the common ancestor stage before the Babylonian/Assyrian split |
| Old Babylonian (OB) | c. 2000 – c. 1600 BCE | Law codes such as Hammurabi's, private letters, mathematical and omen texts; the classical literary base for later periods |
| Middle Babylonian (MB) | c. 1600 – c. 1000 BCE | Kassite-period administrative texts, boundary-stone (*kudurru*) inscriptions; the diplomatic correspondence of the Amarna letters, written in a "peripheral Akkadian," belongs to roughly this linguistic layer |
| Neo-Babylonian (NB) | c. 1000 – c. 600 BCE | Archives of legal and economic documents, royal inscriptions |
| Late Babylonian (LB) | c. 600 BCE – c. 1st century CE | Astronomical diaries, economic texts; the final stage of Akkadian written in cuneiform |

## Periods of the Assyrian Dialect Group

| Period | Approximate dates | Representative genres |
|---|---|---|
| Old Assyrian (OA) | c. 2000 – c. 1600 BCE | [Merchant trade letters and contracts](/en/akkadian/readings/old-assyrian-trade-letter/) from the Anatolian site of Kanesh (Kültepe) |
| Middle Assyrian (MA) | c. 1600 – c. 1000 BCE | The Middle Assyrian Laws, administrative texts |
| Neo-Assyrian (NA) | c. 1000 – c. 600 BCE | [Royal annals and victory inscriptions](/en/akkadian/readings/neo-assyrian-royal-inscription/), royal correspondence, divination reports |

## Standard Babylonian as a Literary Koine

From the Old Babylonian period onward, literary, religious, and scholarly texts came to be written not in the spoken dialect of their own time and place, but in a literary standard known as **Standard Babylonian (SB)**. Standard Babylonian is based on Old Babylonian but incorporates some later linguistic features; crucially, it functioned only as a written register and was never anyone's native spoken language — a genuine literary koine.

The standard version of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the creation epic *Enūma Eliš*, and the great omen-text series are typically composed in Standard Babylonian, even when the surviving manuscript was copied by an Assyrian scribe in the Neo-Assyrian period. This mirrors the role that Literary Chinese played for East Asia or Latin played for medieval Europe: a shared scholarly and literary language, detached from the drift of everyday speech, that transcended both time and regional dialect.

Because of this conservatism, traditional formulas — such as royal titulary — could persist essentially unchanged across centuries. A word like *šarratum*, "queen," for instance, appears in Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian royal inscriptions in almost the same spelling it had in Old Babylonian.

<p class="cuneiform">𒊭𒅈𒊏𒌈</p>

```
šarratum
queen (fem. sg. nominative)
```

## Geography and Dialect, in Summary

Babylonia and Assyria often formed separate, rival kingdoms politically, yet both centers continued to share the same cuneiform scribal culture and the same Standard Babylonian literary norm. As a result, an Akkadian text has to be classified along two independent axes: which period and region's spoken language it reflects (Old Babylonian, Middle Assyrian, and so on), and whether it is composed in the Standard Babylonian literary register.
