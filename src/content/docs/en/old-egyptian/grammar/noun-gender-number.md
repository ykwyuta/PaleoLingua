## Two genders

Old Egyptian nouns belong to one of two grammatical genders: **masculine** and **feminine**. Masculine nouns carry no special ending (they are unmarked), while feminine nouns are formed by adding **-t** to the end of the word.

For example, **nṯr** "god" (masculine) becomes **nṯrt** "goddess" (feminine) simply by adding -t. This is not only a natural-gender distinction (male vs. female beings) but, as in French or German, a grammatical category assigned to inanimate nouns as well.

<p class="hieroglyph">𓈖𓍿𓂋</p>

```
nṯr
"god" (masculine, unmarked)
```

<p class="hieroglyph">𓈖𓍿𓂋𓏏</p>

```
nṯrt
"goddess" (feminine, nṯr + -t)
```

(Where a katakana reading convention is mentioned elsewhere for the Japanese edition of this series, note that it represents a modern Egyptological convention for pronouncing transliterations, not a reconstruction of the actual ancient pronunciation — Egyptian writing does not record vowels.)

## Three numbers

Beyond singular and plural, Old Egyptian has a third number category: the **dual**, used specifically for exactly two of something. This is a feature shared with Semitic languages such as Arabic and Hebrew, and typologically parallels the dual of Classical Greek.

- **Singular**: unmarked
- **Plural**: masculine **-w**, feminine **-wt**
- **Dual**: masculine **-wy**, feminine **-ty**

## The full paradigm of nṯr "god"

Using only the uniliteral (single-consonant) signs n, ṯ, r, t, w, y, all six forms below can be spelled out fully phonetically.

| Form | Transliteration | Hieroglyph | Morpheme analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Masc. singular | nṯr | 𓈖𓍿𓂋 | nṯr (god) | god |
| Fem. singular | nṯrt | 𓈖𓍿𓂋𓏏 | nṯr + -t (fem.) | goddess |
| Masc. plural | nṯrw | 𓈖𓍿𓂋𓅱𓏥 | nṯr + -w (masc. pl.) | gods |
| Fem. plural | nṯrwt | 𓈖𓍿𓂋𓅱𓏏𓏥 | nṯr + -wt (fem. pl.) | goddesses |
| Masc. dual | nṯrwy | 𓈖𓍿𓂋𓅱𓏭 | nṯr + -wy (masc. dual) | two gods |
| Fem. dual | nṯrty | 𓈖𓍿𓂋𓏏𓏭 | nṯr + -ty (fem. dual) | two goddesses |

Notice the sign 𓏥 (Z2, "three strokes") appended after the plural forms. This is a determinative that visually marks plurality regardless of whether -w/-wt is also spelled out phonetically — it lets a reader recognize a plural noun at a glance. The dual is often marked the same way with two strokes (𓏭, Z4, "pair of strokes"). Egyptian number-marking therefore rests on a double system: phonetic spelling of the ending, plus a semantic determinative that flags the grammatical category visually.

## The wider role of feminine -t

The feminine ending -t is not limited to marking feminine nouns; it is also a highly productive derivational suffix used, among other things, to form abstract nouns ("the state of being X"). For now, the key point to master is the basic masculine/feminine opposition crossed with the three-way singular/dual/plural number system.
