Sanskrit compounding (समास samāsa) is extraordinarily productive: multiple words are fused into a single word without intervening case endings, producing compact yet richly allusive expressions. Classical poetry and philosophical prose sometimes feature enormous compounds stringing together ten or more elements. Traditional grammar sorts samāsa into five main types based on differences in meaning and structure.

## Tatpuruṣa (तत्पुरुष): Determinative Compounds

In a tatpuruṣa, the first member is subordinate to the second by a case relationship (genitive, dative, locative, and so on). The meaning and part of speech of the whole compound is determined by the second member (hence tatpuruṣa is called a "head-final" compound type).

```
राजपुरुषः rājapuruṣaḥ
राज rāja (king) + पुरुष puruṣa (man)
"king's man" (i.e., a royal servant)
```

Here, the implicit genitive relationship ("man of the king") between rāja "king" and puruṣa "man" is expressed by compounding, with the case ending omitted. Depending on which case relationship the first member represents, tatpuruṣas are further subdivided into instrumental tatpuruṣa, dative tatpuruṣa, and so on.

## Karmadhāraya (कर्मधारय): Appositional Descriptive Compounds

Karmadhāraya is a subtype of tatpuruṣa in which the first and second members stand not in a case relationship but in an **appositional (adjectival modification)** relationship.

```
नीलोत्पलम् nīlotpalam
नील nīla (blue) + उत्पल utpala (lotus)
"blue lotus"
```

Here the first member, nīla "blue," modifies the second member, utpala "lotus," adjectivally — distinguishing this from an ordinary genitive tatpuruṣa.

## Dvandva (द्वन्द्व): Copulative Compounds

A dvandva places two or more nouns side by side on equal footing, meaning "X and Y." The number (singular, dual, or plural) of the whole compound reflects the number of elements joined.

```
रामलक्ष्मणौ rāmalakṣmaṇau
राम rāma (Rāma) + लक्ष्मण lakṣmaṇa (Lakṣmaṇa)
"Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa" (dual, since it names two people)
```

```
मातापितरौ mātāpitarau
मातृ mātṛ (mother) + पितृ pitṛ (father)
"mother and father" (parents)
```

## Bahuvrīhi (बहुव्रीहि): Possessive Compounds

The defining feature of a bahuvrīhi is that the compound as a whole does not refer to either of its members, but instead functions as an **adjective describing something else entirely** — an external referent outside the compound (hence the term "exocentric compound").

```
पीताम्बरः pītāmbaraḥ
पीत pīta (yellow) + अम्बर ambara (garment)
Literally "yellow garment," but as a bahuvrīhi it functions as an adjective/epithet meaning "one whose garment is yellow" = Kṛṣṇa (the god)
```

The word pītāmbara does not itself mean "garment." It functions strictly as an adjective referring to a person outside the compound (in this case Kṛṣṇa) who "has a yellow garment." This is the crucial difference from a tatpuruṣa or karmadhāraya, where the compound itself carries the core meaning. The very term bahuvrīhi is a self-illustrating example: it literally means "having much (bahu) rice (vrīhi)," i.e., "a wealthy person" — itself a bahuvrīhi compound.

## Avyayībhāva (अव्ययीभाव): Adverbial Compounds

An avyayībhāva places an indeclinable (an adverb or preposition-like particle) as the first member, and the compound as a whole functions as an **indeclinable adverb**.

```
यथाशक्ति yathāśakti
यथा yathā (according to) + शक्ति śakti (ability)
"according to (one's) ability" (adverb)
```

Unlike the other four compound types (all of which decline as ordinary nouns or adjectives), the whole compound here is invariant, fixed in the neuter nominative/accusative singular.

## A Summary of the Five Compound Types

| Type | Relationship between first and second member | Grammatical/semantic center of the whole compound |
|---|---|---|
| Tatpuruṣa | First member subordinate to second via a case relationship | The second member (a noun) |
| Karmadhāraya | First member adjectivally modifies second (apposition) | The second member (a noun) |
| Dvandva | First and second members coordinated equally | The combined set of both members |
| Bahuvrīhi | Both members together describe a referent outside the compound | An external referent (exocentric) |
| Avyayībhāva | An indeclinable first member modifies the second | An indeclinable adverb |
