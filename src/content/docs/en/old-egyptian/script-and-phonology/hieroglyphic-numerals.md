## A decimal system without place value

Ancient Egypt's numeral system worked very differently from the positional numeral system we use today (where, as in Arabic numerals, the same digit "1" can mean 1, 10, or 100 depending on its position). Egyptian numerals were a **non-positional decimal system**: each power of ten — 1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000, and 1,000,000 — had its own dedicated sign, and a number was formed simply by **repeating each sign as many times as needed and adding the values together**.

## The sign for 1: a simple repeated stroke

The most basic numeral sign is a single vertical stroke representing 1 (Gardiner code Z1).

<p class="hieroglyph">𓏤</p>

```
1
a single vertical stroke representing the number "1"
```

The numbers 2 through 9 are formed simply by **repeating** this stroke the appropriate number of times — three strokes for 3, seven strokes for 7, and so on, built purely by repetition and addition. This is a fundamentally different approach from positional notation: to write "23," there's no need to combine distinct "2" and "3" symbols by position — you simply lay out however many signs are needed for each power of ten.

## Powers of ten above 1: dedicated signs (described by Gardiner code only)

The powers of ten — 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000, and 1,000,000 — each had their own dedicated pictorial sign, quite unlike the plain vertical stroke used for 1. These glyphs are not included in this article's verified sign set, so no Unicode character is shown for them here — only their Gardiner code and what they originally depicted.

| Power of ten | Gardiner code | Original depiction |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | V20 | A heel bone (hobble) shape |
| 100 | V1 | A coiled rope |
| 1,000 | M12 | A lotus flower |
| 10,000 | D50 | A bent finger |
| 100,000 | I8 | A tadpole (frog larva) |
| 1,000,000 | C11 | The god Heh kneeling with arms raised (Heh symbolized infinity/eternity) |

## Numbers built by addition

To write an Egyptian number, each power-of-ten sign is repeated as many times as needed, and the reader simply adds up all the values represented. For example, "1,000,000 + 100,000 + 100 + 10 + 3" would be written by laying out signs from the largest power down to the smallest, and the reader counts up each group of repeated signs to arrive at the total. To anyone accustomed to positional notation, having to draw the same sign over and over may seem inefficient, but the system worked perfectly well for displaying quantities clearly and visually on monuments and administrative records.
