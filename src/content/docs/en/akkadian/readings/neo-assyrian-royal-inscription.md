## Background: What Is a Neo-Assyrian Royal Inscription?

Between the 9th and 7th centuries BCE, the kings of Neo-Assyria — Tiglath-Pileser III, Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal, and others — built an empire of unprecedented scale centered on northern Mesopotamia, and left behind an enormous number of inscriptions commemorating their achievements. These survive in many media: relief-carved stone slabs lining palace walls, annals inscribed on clay cylinders and prisms, and dedicatory texts buried in building foundations.

The purpose of these inscriptions falls broadly into two categories. First, **commemorating temple and palace building projects** — the king boasting of having restored or expanded a temple for a particular god. Second, **recording military campaigns** — listing the cities subdued and enemies conquered, emphasizing the king's martial prowess and the gods' favor. These should be understood not as objective "historical records" in the modern sense, but as formal propaganda documents projecting royal ideology both at home and abroad.

## The Titulary Formula

Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions typically open with a **titulary** — a list of the king's epithets. Titles are stacked up in a pattern of "great king, mighty king, king of X," proclaiming the king's legitimacy and majesty. Below is one of the most basic such combinations, shown with verified cuneiform.

<p class="cuneiform">𒊬𒊒 𒆗𒉡 𒊬 𒈠𒀜 𒀸𒋩</p>

```
šar-ru    dan-nu     šar        ma-at        aš-šur
king      mighty     king-(cst) land-(cst)   Aššur
```

"Mighty king, king of the land of Aššur" — *šarru dannu* "mighty king" is followed by the phrase *šar māt Aššur* "king of the land of Aššur." Here *šar* (the **construct state** of *šarru*, with its case ending dropped) directly modifies the following *māt* (construct state of *mātu*, "land, country"), and *māt* in turn combines with the place name *Aššur* (both the Assyrian capital and the name of its patron god) to express possession: "the land of Aššur." Note also that Neo-Assyrian has already lost the word-final mimation (the -m of forms like -um) found in earlier Old Babylonian, so we get *šarru* rather than *šarrum*.

## Titulary Vocabulary

| Transliteration | Cuneiform | Morphological analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| šar-ru | 𒊬𒊒 | šarru (stem šarr- + nominative -u, no mimation) | king |
| dan-nu | 𒆗𒉡 | dannu (adjective stem dann- + nominative -u) | mighty, strong |

## The Formal Structure of the Genre

Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions generally follow a set sequence:

| Component | Content |
|---|---|
| Titulary | The king's list of titles and epithets ("great king," "mighty king," "king of X," etc.) |
| Divine mandate | Reference to the gods (Aššur foremost) who chose and appointed the king |
| Genealogy | A statement affirming succession from the previous king |
| Campaign narrative | An annalistic account listing conquered cities and enemies, in chronological or geographic order |
| Building narrative | A description of temple/palace construction or restoration, often naming materials and techniques used |
| Curse and blessing | Blessings for future kings who respect the inscription, and curses on anyone who damages or alters it |

Thanks to this highly formulaic structure, scholars can partially reconstruct even fragmentary inscriptions. While the specific combination of titles or the phrasing of campaign narratives varies somewhat from king to king, the basic "template" was preserved with remarkable stability across centuries.
