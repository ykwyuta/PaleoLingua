## What is a determinative?

A determinative is a silent classifier sign, never itself pronounced, that tells the reader which semantic category the adjacent word belongs to — deity, place, wooden object, profession, woman, and so on. Cuneiform signs normally stand for a word or a syllable, but determinatives are the one exception: they function purely as unread "labels." By convention, transliteration marks them as a superscript.

Where a determinative goes — before or after the word it classifies — depends on which one it is. Let's look at six of the most common.

## Before divine names: `d` (DINGIR)

Divine names are preceded by the determinative `d` (from the Sumerian logogram DINGIR, "god"). A good example is the name of the sun-god Šamaš, one of this site's two mascot characters.

<p class="cuneiform">𒀭𒌓</p>

```
dUTU
Šamaš (the sun-god) — d is a divine-name determinative, UTU is the logogram for "sun"
```

The determinative `d` is not pronounced when reading aloud — the name is read simply as "Šamaš."

## After place names: `ki`

Place names (cities, countries) are followed by the determinative `ki` (from Sumerian *ki*, "land, place"). Take the name of Aššur, the central city of Assyria:

<p class="cuneiform">𒀸𒋩𒆠</p>

```
aš-šurki
Aššur (a city name) — ki is a place-name determinative (silent)
```

## Before wooden objects: `giš`

Objects made of wood are preceded by the determinative `giš` (from the Sumerian logogram GIŠ, "wood, tree"). The word `kussûm` "chair, throne" is often spelled with the Sumerogram `GU.ZA` (originally a Sumerian word associated with "lap," used as the logogram for a chair) plus this determinative.

<p class="cuneiform">𒄑𒄖𒍝</p>

```
giš GU.ZA
kussûm "chair, throne" — giš is a determinative marking a wooden object (silent)
```

## Before professions: `lu2`

Words for people and occupations are preceded by the determinative `lu2` (from the Sumerian logogram LU2, "person"). The word `nappāḫum` "smith" is often spelled with the Sumerogram `SIMUG` (the logogram for "smith") plus this determinative.

<p class="cuneiform">𒇽𒌣</p>

```
lu2 SIMUG
nappāḫum "smith" — lu2 is a determinative marking a person or profession (silent)
```

## Before women's names and words: `munus`

Women's personal names, and words denoting a female referent, are preceded by the determinative `munus` (from the Sumerian logogram MUNUS/SAL, "woman"). Adding `munus` to `LUGAL` ("king," šarrum) produces the word for "queen."

<p class="cuneiform">𒊩𒈗</p>

```
MUNUS.LUGAL
šarratum "queen" — munus is a determinative marking female gender (silent), added to LUGAL "king"
```

Spelled syllabically, this word is `ša-ar-ra-tum`.

<p class="cuneiform">𒊭𒅈𒊏𒌈</p>

```
ša-ar-ra-tum
šarratum "queen" (fem. nom. sg.; -atum is the feminine ending)
```

## Before city names: `uru`

City names are often preceded by the determinative `uru` (from the Sumerian logogram URU, "city"), as an alternative to (or alongside) the `ki` suffix. The same city Aššur seen above with a trailing `ki` could, depending on period and scribal habit, also be written with a leading `uru`:

<p class="cuneiform">𒌷𒀸𒋩</p>

```
uru aš-šur
Aššur (a city name) — uru is a city-name determinative (silent)
```

Both conventions — trailing `ki` and leading `uru` — were in wide use for the same city name; the leading `uru` becomes especially common in Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian documents.
