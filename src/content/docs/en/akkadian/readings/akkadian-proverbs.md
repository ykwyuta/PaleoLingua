## Background: Proverb Collections and the Wisdom-Literature Tradition

In Mesopotamian scribal schools — the **edubba**, Sumerian for "tablet house" — apprentice scribes learning to write cuneiform copied out set phrases and proverbs over and over as part of their training. Out of this practice grew large **proverb collections** gathering Sumerian sayings, and later, **bilingual editions** pairing the Sumerian original with an Akkadian translation line by line. These belong to the broader genre known as **wisdom literature**: observations about life, conduct, and human relationships condensed into short, memorable form.

Most proverbs deliver their point through brief parallel couplets, striking comparisons, or unexpected juxtapositions. Because they were copied as part of scribal training, most surviving exemplars come from student practice tablets.

## Example Proverbs

Below are two short sentences constructed to illustrate the typical grammar and subject matter of this genre (composed to reflect the grammatical patterns and themes — domestic piety, the value of friendship — found in the real Sumerian/Akkadian proverb collections edited by scholars such as Bendt Alster, rather than a verbatim quotation of one specific numbered tablet).

### Example 1: A saying about purity and piety

<p class="cuneiform">𒁉𒌈 𒂖𒇻 𒀀𒈾 𒄿𒅆 𒁕𒁍</p>

```
bi-tum    el-lu    a-na   i-lim   ṭa-bu
house-NOM pure     to     god-GEN good, pleasing
```

"A pure house is pleasing to a god" — this kind of saying, linking domestic purity to piety toward the gods, reflects a recurring Mesopotamian religious value: that ritual purity secures divine favor.

### Example 2: A saying about friendship

<p class="cuneiform">𒅁𒊒𒌝 𒁕𒁍𒌝 𒆠𒈠 𒀀𒄭𒅎</p>

```
ib-ru-um    ṭa-bu-um    ki-ma   a-ḫi-im
friend-NOM  good-NOM    like    brother-GEN
```

"A good friend is like a brother" — comparing friendship to a blood relationship is a recurring theme in the Sumerian and Akkadian proverb collections, echoing a broader concern in this literature with friendship, loyalty, and the risk of betrayal.

## Literary and Cultural Significance

Unlike formal documents such as royal inscriptions or law codes, proverbs carry a distinctive source value: they preserve everyday moral attitudes, social relationships, and practical wisdom. Their frequent leaps of metaphor, humor, or wry irony give a rare glimpse into the everyday sensibility of Mesopotamian people. And the fact that proverb collections were used as school texts shows that this genre did more than teach writing — it also transmitted a society's shared values to the next generation.
