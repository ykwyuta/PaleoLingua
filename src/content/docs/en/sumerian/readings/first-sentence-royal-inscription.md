## The royal inscription genre

When Sumerian kings built or restored a temple, city wall, or other major structure, they had inscriptions carved into clay or stone to commemorate the project. These "royal (building) inscriptions" stayed remarkably formulaic across several centuries, most of them repeating a basic pattern: "So-and-so built such-and-such for a god or goddess." That formulaic quality makes them an ideal first text for a beginner reading something close to a genuine primary source.

The sentence below is a simplified teaching example modeled on phrasing that recurs across many actual royal inscriptions (such as those of Gudea of Lagash and Ur-Namma of Ur) — it is not a verbatim quotation of one specific inscription.

## Example sentence

<p class="cuneiform">𒌨𒀭𒇉   𒈗𒂊   𒂍𒐐   𒈬𒆕</p>

```
ur-dnamma   lugal-e   e2-ninnu   mu-du3
Ur-Namma    king-ERG  Eninnu     he-built
```

**Word-by-word gloss**

| Word | Cuneiform | Morphological breakdown | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ur-`d`namma | 𒌨𒀭𒇉 | proper noun | Ur-Namma (personal name) |
| lugal-e | 𒈗𒂊 | lugal ("king") + -e (ergative) | the king (as agent) |
| e2-ninnu | 𒂍𒐐 | proper noun (temple name) | the Eninnu (a temple name, often glossed "house of fifty") |
| mu-du3 | 𒈬𒆕 | mu- (ventive prefix) + du3 ("to build") | he built |

**Translation**: "King Ur-Namma built the Eninnu temple."

## Grammatical points to notice

- As in `lugal-e`, the subject of a transitive verb (`du3`, "to build") takes the ergative suffix `-e` (see [Ergative and Absolutive Case](/en/sumerian/grammar/sumerian-ergative/)).
- `e2-ninnu` (the object) is absolutive, appearing unmarked with no case suffix.
- The verb `mu-du3` sits at the very end of the sentence, exactly as expected from [SOV word order](/en/sumerian/grammar/word-order-sov/).
