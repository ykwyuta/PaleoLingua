## Borrowing a Script: From Sumerian to Akkadian

Akkadian entered history without a native script of its own. Its speakers adopted **cuneiform**, the writing system their Sumerian neighbors had spent centuries developing, and pressed it into service for a structurally very different, East Semitic language. By the later third millennium BCE (around 2350 BCE, the era of the Akkadian Empire), texts already appear that apply Sumerian's writing system to Akkadian.

The result of this borrowing is a **mixed writing system** combining three kinds of signs:

1. **Syllabic signs (syllabograms)** — phonetic signs representing a syllable such as a consonant plus a vowel.
2. **Sumerograms (logograms)** — Sumerian word-signs reused wholesale to represent the corresponding Akkadian word.
3. **Determinatives** — silent classifier signs that are never pronounced but signal the semantic category of the word next to them.

## Syllabic Signs (Syllabograms)

Akkadian syllabic signs fall into four basic shapes: V (vowel alone), CV (consonant + vowel), VC (vowel + consonant), and CVC (consonant + vowel + consonant).

| Type | Cuneiform | Transliteration | Meaning / Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| V | 𒀀 | a | A vowel-only syllable |
| CV | 𒊭 | ša | Consonant + vowel |
| CV | 𒊒 | ru | Consonant + vowel |
| VC | 𒅈 | ar | Vowel + consonant |
| VC | 𒅁 | ip | Vowel + consonant |
| CVC | 𒌈 | tum | Consonant + vowel + consonant |
| CVC | 𒈝 | lum | Consonant + vowel + consonant |

Scribes combined such signs to spell out polysyllabic words. For example, the word for "king," *šarrum*, is spelled with a **broken (syllable-by-syllable) spelling**: CV `ša`, VC `ar`, CV `ru`, and CVC `um`, written `ša-ar-ru-um`.

<p class="cuneiform">𒊭𒅈𒊒𒌝</p>

```
ša-ar-ru-um
king (masc. sg. nominative)
```

## Sumerograms (Logograms)

Akkadian scribes frequently wrote common words using Sumerian word-signs, or Sumerograms, conventionally rendered in transliteration with small capitals. For instance, instead of the syllabic spelling `ša-ar-ru-um`, "king" could be written with the single Sumerian logogram **LUGAL**.

<p class="cuneiform">𒈗</p>

```
LUGAL (read šarrum)
king
```

Sumerograms save the scribe writing effort but do not display how a word is inflected (its case, number, person, and so on) on the surface of the text. For this reason, teaching materials that need to show case endings or verbal affixes generally prefer syllabic spellings like `ša-ar-ru-um`, which spell out every morpheme.

## Determinatives

Determinatives are silent signs that tell the reader the semantic category — divine name, place name, wooden object, profession, and so on — of the word immediately before or after them. In transliteration they are written as superscripts.

| Cuneiform | Transliteration (superscript) | Position and use |
|---|---|---|
| 𒀭 | ᵈ (dingir) | Precedes divine names, marking the following word as a god's name |
| 𒆠 | ki | Follows place names, marking the preceding word as a place name |
| 𒄑 | ᵍⁱˢ (giš) | Precedes words for wooden objects or implements |
| 𒇽 | ˡᵘ² (lu₂) | Precedes words for professions or social roles |

Unlike syllabic signs and Sumerograms, a determinative is never voiced when the text is read aloud — it functions purely as a silent visual annotation. The ᵈ before a divine name, for instance, is not pronounced; it simply flags "this is a god's name" for the reader's eye.

## A Semitic Language in a Sumerian-Built Script

Sumerian is an agglutinative language with relatively simple syllable structure. Akkadian, by contrast, is a classic Semitic language built on **triconsonantal roots** — a skeleton of three (occasionally two or four) consonants that carries the core meaning, with vowel patterns and affixes layered on to express grammatical function. Applying a script designed for Sumerian's structure to Akkadian's structure produces several mismatches.

- **Consonant clusters are awkward to write.** Because cuneiform syllabic signs are essentially built around vowels (V, CV, VC, CVC), they cannot directly represent the consonant clusters that are common in Semitic morphology. Scribes worked around this by inserting extra vowels or combining signs creatively.
- **Emphatic consonants are not always distinguished at the level of the sign.** Sumerian lacked the plain/emphatic contrasts of Akkadian (*t* vs. *ṭ*, *s* vs. *ṣ*, and so on), so a single cuneiform sign can be read as more than one distinct Akkadian consonant. The sign below, for example, can be transliterated as three different sounds — voiceless, voiced, or emphatic — despite being a single, identical glyph.

| Cuneiform | Transliteration | Corresponding sound |
|---|---|---|
| 𒋫 | ta | Voiceless dental t |
| 𒋫 | da | Voiced dental d |
| 𒋫 | ṭa | Emphatic (glottalized/pharyngealized) dental ṭ |

  The reader has to rely on context and knowledge of the word's root to decide which of `ta`, `da`, or `ṭa` a given occurrence of this sign represents.

- **Vowel length is not always marked.** Akkadian distinguishes short and long vowels (see [The Akkadian Phoneme Inventory](/en/akkadian/script-and-phonology/akkadian-phoneme-inventory/)), but ordinary syllabic spelling frequently leaves vowel length unmarked; when scribes wanted to show a long vowel explicitly, they could double up vowel signs in what is called "plene" spelling.

Despite these constraints, Akkadian scribes kept this borrowed writing system running for well over a thousand years, using it to produce everything from law codes to letters to literary epics.
